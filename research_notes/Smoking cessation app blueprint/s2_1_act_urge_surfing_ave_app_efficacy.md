## Q1. ACT for smoking cessation: the iCanQuit trial, WebQuit, later Fred Hutch trials, which ACT processes are taught, and the mediation evidence

### Takeaway
In the largest app trial to date (iCanQuit, n=2,415), the ACT-based app beat a guideline-based app (QuitGuide) on 12-month 30-day point-prevalence abstinence (PPA): 28.2% vs 21.1%, OR 1.49 (95% CI 1.22–1.83). The effect was **mediated by engagement, which increased acceptance of physical sensations and emotions** that cue smoking. It was **not** mediated by acceptance of thoughts or by valued living. The earlier web version (WebQuit vs Smokefree.gov, n=2,637) showed **no difference** (24% vs 26%). So ACT content alone is not sufficient: the delivery format and the level of engagement matter.

### Cited Findings
**iCanQuit main trial (Bricker JB, Watson NL, Mull KE, Sullivan BM, Heffner JL. "Efficacy of Smartphone Applications for Smoking Cessation: A Randomized Clinical Trial." JAMA Intern Med 2020;180(11):1472–1480. DOI 10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4055; NCT02724462)**
- Design: 2,415 adult cigarette smokers randomized to iCanQuit (ACT) or QuitGuide (US Clinical Practice Guideline-based), each used for 12 months. Follow-up surveys at 3, 6 and 12 months. — [Fred Hutch news release, Sept 2020](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/releases/2020/09/fred-hutch-led-clinical-trial-shows-new-smartphone-app-helps-smokers-quit.html); [JAMA IM DOI](https://dx.doi.org/10.1001/jamainternmed.2020.4055)
- Primary outcome: 30-day PPA at 12 months, **28.2% iCanQuit vs 21.1% QuitGuide**. Participants were "1.49 times more likely to quit" (OR 1.49; 95% CI 1.22–1.83). — [Fred Hutch](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/releases/2020/09/fred-hutch-led-clinical-trial-shows-new-smartphone-app-helps-smokers-quit.html); [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT02724462](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT02724462)
- NNT = 14 (95% CI 8.5 to 28.8). — [AAFP POEM summary, Am Fam Physician 2021](https://www.aafp.org/afp/2021/0401/p441)
- How the content differs: "iCanQuit teaches users to accept urges and let them pass," while QuitGuide "emphasizes avoiding high-risk situations and keeping hands and mouths busy." — [Fred Hutch](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/releases/2020/09/fred-hutch-led-clinical-trial-shows-new-smartphone-app-helps-smokers-quit.html); [Psychiatric News 2020](https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.pn.2020.11a6)
- iCanQuit structure (as described in the iCanQuit secondary-analysis papers):
  - **8 levels built on two ACT core processes**: (1) acceptance of cravings to smoke, and (2) aligning with the core values that motivate a smoke-free life.
  - Levels 2–4 hold **26 exercises teaching acceptance of cravings, emotions and thoughts** that trigger smoking.
  - Later levels hold **25 exercises** on building smoke-free activities and on coping with withdrawal, slips, depression and potential weight gain.
  - **If a user records a lapse, the program encourages, but does not require, setting a new quit date and returning to the first four levels.**
  - — [Santiago-Torres et al., Depression and Anxiety 2024 (DOI 10.1155/2024/1055801)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2024/1055801); [PubMed 36683573 (high-dependence analysis)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36683573)

**Mediation / mechanism evidence**
- Bricker JB et al. "Mechanisms of Smartphone Apps for Cigarette Smoking Cessation: Results of a Serial Mediation Model From the iCanQuit Randomized Trial." JMIR mHealth uHealth 2021;9(11):e32847 (PMID 34751662):
  - A significant **serial mediation**: iCanQuit → engagement (total logins, total minutes, unique days of use) → increased acceptance of internal cues to smoke from baseline to 3 months → cessation.
  - Mediation ran through **acceptance of physical sensations and emotions, not acceptance of thoughts**.
  - There was **no evidence of mediation through valued living**.
  - — [PMC8663588](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8663588/); [JMIR](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2021/11/e32847)
- Dual users (combustible plus e-cigarettes), secondary analysis: across the full parent-trial sample there was a "significant total effect as well as a robust serial mediation of iCanQuit's effect on cigarette smoking through app engagement, leading to increased acceptance of cravings." — [PMC12360788 / PMID 38098199](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12360788/)
- Mental-health-disorder subgroup: iCanQuit's efficacy was mediated by acceptance of emotions that trigger smoking and by app engagement. — [Santiago-Torres 2024, Depression and Anxiety](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2024/1055801)
- WebQuit, Black adults: willingness to accept urges to smoke mediated the effect on 12-month abstinence. — [PMC11742261](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11742261/)

**iCanQuit subgroup / secondary analyses**
- Adults with mental health disorders (770 iCanQuit vs 785 QuitGuide): cessation, engagement and satisfaction were all significantly greater with iCanQuit. — [Santiago-Torres et al. 2024, Depression and Anxiety, DOI 10.1155/2024/1055801](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1155/2024/1055801)
- High nicotine dependence: 30-day abstinence at 12 months 24% vs 17%, OR 1.47. — [PubMed 36683573](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36683573)
- Black adults: complete-case 30-day abstinence at 12 months 28% vs 20% (OR 1.60). The association was similar but non-significant under missing-as-smoking imputation. — [Santiago-Torres et al., Addiction 2022, DOI 10.1111/add.15721](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/add.15721)
- Rural adults: significantly higher short-term quit rates and descriptively higher long-term rates with iCanQuit. — [Preventive Medicine 2022](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0091743522000561)
- Low-income adults: a secondary analysis exists (Drug Alcohol Depend). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376871621007535)
- A secondary analysis on whether medications increase the efficacy of the digital interventions also exists. — [PMC10932808](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10932808/)

**WebQuit (Bricker JB, Mull KE, McClure JB, Watson NL, Heffner JL. "Improving quit rates of web-delivered interventions for smoking cessation: full-scale randomized trial of WebQuit.org versus Smokefree.gov." Addiction 2018;113(5):914–923. PMID 29235186; PMC5930021; NCT01812278)**
- Design: two-arm, stratified, double-blind RCT (WebQuit n=1,319; Smokefree.gov n=1,318). Adults smoking ≥5 cigarettes/day, recruited March 2014–August 2015. 12-month retention 88% (2,309/2,637). — [PubMed 29235186](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29235186/); [PMC5930021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5930021/)
- 30-day PPA at 12 months: **24% (278/1,141) vs 26% (305/1,168); OR 0.91 (95% CI 0.76–1.10); p=0.334**. Bayes factor 0.17 ("substantial" evidence of no difference). — [contextualscience.org abstract](https://contextualscience.org/publications/improving_quit_rates_webdelivered_interventions_smoking_cessation_full_scale); [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29235186/)
- The 2013 web pilot (n=222) had shown 23% vs 10% at 3 months, which the full trial did not replicate. — [contextualscience.org](https://contextualscience.org/publications/pilot_randomized_controlled_trial_webbased_acceptance_commitment_therapy_smoking)
- WebQuit subgroups: Black adults 34% vs 29% (n.s.). — [PMC11742261](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11742261/). Hispanic/Latinx 40% vs 25%, p=0.037 (n=222). — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2211335522002595)

**Later Fred Hutch / ACT-adjacent work (2023–2026)**
- **QuitBot** (chatbot "Ellen", Fred Hutch HABIT lab), pilot RCT vs NCI SmokefreeTXT. Among participants who viewed all 42 days of content, 3-month 30-day PPA was 63% (39/62) vs 38.5% (45/117), OR 2.58, with 96% retention (complete-case pilot data). — [JMIR mHealth uHealth 2024;12:e57318](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2024/1/e57318). The full-scale trial is NCT04308759. In 2024 GeekWire reported data collection from more than 1,600 users. **No Phase III results were found.** — [GeekWire 2024](https://www.geekwire.com/2024/fred-hutch-researchers-release-free-app-to-help-smokers-quit-with-help-from-a-chatbot-named-ellen/); [Fred Hutch tech page](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/investors/business-development/available-technologies/technology-details.fh_bds_technology_id_23-151.quitbot-an-innovative-chat-app-to-assist-smoking-cessation.html)
- **ACT on Vaping** (Heffner JL et al., pilot RCT, Nicotine Tob Res 2025, DOI 10.1093/ntr/ntaf112; PMC13101998):
  - Design: young adults aged 18–30, n=61 (31 app vs 30 incentivized text-message control), 3-month outcomes.
  - Satisfaction averaged 3.8/5. Attrition was ~23% in the app arm vs ~3% in control.
  - The authors recommend a fully powered trial.
  - The full trial NCT06909500 has estimated enrollment of 1,178; no results yet.
  - — [NTR](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntaf112); [PMC13101998](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13101998/); [NCT06909500](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT06909500)
- ACT for tobacco cessation among psychiatric partial-hospital patients: a registered trial (NCT03911960) exists. — [protocol PDF](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/60/NCT03911960/Prot_003.pdf)
- 2025 meta-analysis of ACT for smoking cessation (Fan et al., Worldviews on Evidence-Based Nursing 2025, DOI 10.1111/wvn.70086; Bricker reportedly a co-author): ACT vs all control types, **RR 1.48 (95% CI 1.03–2.14), I²=81%** immediately post-intervention. ACT was also significant at short-term follow-up. — [Wiley](https://sigmapubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/wvn.70086)

**ACT processes and metaphors (paraphrased; [PK], from the ACT canon, not verified this session)**
- The six ACT processes are acceptance, cognitive defusion, present-moment contact, self-as-context, values and committed action. — [PK] Hayes SC, Strosahl KD, Wilson KG. *Acceptance and Commitment Therapy: The Process and Practice of Mindful Change*, 2nd ed. Guilford, 2012; [Hayes et al. 2006 Behav Res Ther, DOI 10.1016/j.brat.2005.06.006](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.brat.2005.06.006)
- **Passengers on the Bus** (Hayes et al. 1999/2012), paraphrase: you drive a bus toward what matters. Unruly passengers (urges, "just one", irritability) shout directions. You can't throw them off, but you don't have to obey them; you keep driving with them on board. — [PK, Hayes et al. 2012, Guilford](https://www.guilford.com/books/Acceptance-and-Commitment-Therapy/Hayes-Strosahl-Wilson/9781462528943)
- **Tug-of-war with a monster**, paraphrase: struggling with a craving is like pulling a rope against a monster across a pit. The move is to drop the rope, not to win. — [PK, same source](https://www.guilford.com/books/Acceptance-and-Commitment-Therapy/Hayes-Strosahl-Wilson/9781462528943)
- **Chessboard**, paraphrase: you are the board that holds the black and white pieces (thoughts and feelings for and against smoking), not a player who has to defeat one side. — [PK, same source]
- **Leaves on a Stream**: place each thought on a leaf and watch it float past without grabbing or pushing it. — [PK, Hayes SC, *Get Out of Your Mind and Into Your Life*, New Harbinger 2005](https://www.newharbinger.com/9781572244252/get-out-of-your-mind-and-into-your-life/)
- **"I'm having the thought that…"** defusion stem (e.g., "I need a cigarette" becomes "I'm having the thought that I need a cigarette"). — [PK, Harris R, *ACT Made Simple*, New Harbinger 2009/2019]
- Hayes discusses iCanQuit and its ACT rationale in a 2020 blog post. — [Psychology Today, "How to Quit Smoking: Why Science Matters"](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/get-out-of-your-mind/202009/how-to-quit-smoking-why-science-matters)

### Inferences
- **Lead with body- and emotion-focused acceptance.** The only process the iCanQuit mediation confirmed was acceptance of *physical sensations and emotions*. Thought acceptance and valued living did not mediate. So the app's core repeated drill should be "notice and allow the bodily urge", with emotion acceptance second. Defusion and values are still worth teaching for motivation and onboarding, but their causal role is unproven. Expect payoff mainly from the sensation and emotion drills.
- **Design for engagement.** Engagement (logins, minutes, unique days) is the first link in the serial mediation. WebQuit, with ACT content on the web, showed no effect, while iCanQuit on a smartphone did. Repeated, short, on-demand practice is likely what drives the effect, more than any single lesson. Use micro-exercises of 30–180 s that can be launched from a notification or home-screen widget at the moment of craving.
- **Proposed micro-exercises.** These are my own designs, adapted from the paraphrased metaphors above. None has been tested as a standalone app module.
  1. *Bus Driver (60–90 s)*: the user picks 2–3 "passengers" from chips ("Just one won't hurt", "I'm stressed", "I'm bored", restlessness in hands). They appear as bubbles in the back of a bus. The user taps "Keep driving" toward their chosen value card. The bubbles stay visible but shrink in importance. Closing line: "They can ride along. You're still driving."
  2. *Drop the Rope (45 s)*: an animated rope with a craving "monster". The user pulls (the struggle animation intensifies), then taps "Drop the rope": the monster remains, the user's hands are free, and a prompt asks "What will you do with your hands now?" (one valued action).
  3. *Thought Stream (60 s)*: the user types or picks a smoking thought. It goes onto a leaf and floats away. No delete or "zap" button, to avoid a suppression framing.
  4. *Name It (20 s)*: a text transform. The user's thought "I need a smoke" is rewritten on screen as "I'm noticing the thought that I need a smoke", optionally with a second step: "…and I can choose what I do next."
  5. *Shape the Craving (60 s)*: the user gives the urge a location, size, colour, temperature and movement. This is an ACT-style physicalizing exercise and maps directly onto the sensation-acceptance mediator.
  6. *Values Compass (onboarding, 2–3 min; refresh weekly)*: the user picks top values (family, health, money, freedom, being a role model). Then a daily "one small step" committed action is linked to the chosen value.
  7. *The Bear Demo (onboarding, 60 s)*: a white-bear experiential (see Q2), using a neutral object rather than a cigarette.
- **Copy the lapse rule.** iCanQuit's lapse rule ("encourage, don't require, a new quit date and a return to the acceptance levels") is a ready-made, trial-tested pattern for the blueprint's lapse protocol (Q6).

## Q2. Ironic process theory and thought suppression of smoking thoughts: implications for UI copy

### Takeaway
Suppressing a thought tends to produce later **rebound**. In smokers, suppressing smoking thoughts reduced smoking during the suppression week but led to **significantly more smoking the following week** (Erskine et al. 2010). Copy such as "Don't think about smoking", "Fight the urge" or "Push it away" is therefore contraindicated. Acceptance/allowing language is the evidence-aligned alternative.

### Cited Findings
- Wegner DM, Schneider DJ, Carter SR, White TL. "Paradoxical effects of thought suppression." J Pers Soc Psychol 1987;53(1):5–13, DOI 10.1037/0022-3514.53.1.5:
  - Participants trying not to think of a white bear reported the thought anyway (verbal mentions plus bell rings).
  - Participants who had first suppressed later showed **more** bear thoughts when allowed to express them than those who had been asked to think about the bear from the start. The abstract suggests suppression may produce "the very obsession or preoccupation that it is directed against."
  - — [original PDF, Harvard](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Wegner,Schneider,Carter,&White%201987.pdf); [Simply Psychology summary](https://www.simplypsychology.org/ironic-process-theory-white-bear-experiment.html)
- A secondary summary reports that a single focused distracter (e.g., "red Volkswagen") eliminated the paradoxical effect. Not verified against the paper. — [Simply Psychology](https://www.simplypsychology.org/ironic-process-theory-white-bear-experiment.html)
- [PK] Wegner DM. "Ironic processes of mental control." Psychol Rev 1994;101(1):34–52, DOI [10.1037/0033-295X.101.1.34](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-295X.101.1.34). The paper proposes an intentional operating process plus an ironic monitoring process that searches for the unwanted thought. Under cognitive load or stress the monitor dominates, so the unwanted thought becomes *more* accessible.
- **Erskine JAK, Georgiou GJ, Kvavilashvili L.** "I suppress, therefore I smoke: effects of thought suppression on smoking behavior." Psychol Sci 2010;21(9):1225–1230, DOI 10.1177/0956797610378687:
  - 85 smokers (mean age 31), none trying to quit, followed over 3 weeks. In week 2, one group suppressed smoking thoughts, one group expressed them, and a control group did neither.
  - **The suppression group smoked less during the suppression week but significantly more than the other groups in week 3** (behavioural rebound).
  - — [BPS Research Digest](https://www.bps.org.uk/research-digest/stubbing-out-thoughts-smoking-leads-smokers-end-smoking-more); [reference listing](https://www.scirp.org/(S(lz5mqp453edsnp55rrgjct55.))/reference/referencespapers.aspx?referenceid=1625869); [BPS Psychologist "Thoughts on suppression"](https://www.bps.org.uk/psychologist/thoughts-suppression)
- Erskine et al. 2012 follow-up (Psychopharmacology, n=93) examined whether suppression increases desire to smoke and withdrawal. The results were inconsistent with prior findings. The authors suggest suppression may increase later smoking without the effect being mediated by desire. — [Springer, DOI 10.1007/s00213-011-2391-4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s00213-011-2391-4)
- Sayers WM, Sayette MA. "Suppression on your own terms." Psychol Sci 2013, DOI 10.1177/0956797613479977. Smokers who showed suppression-related facial expressions during cue exposure subsequently **valued smoking more**. — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797613479977)
- A 2019/2020 meta-analysis, "Ironic Effects of Thought Suppression: A Meta-Analysis", exists. Its effect sizes were not retrieved. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/332883543_Ironic_Effects_of_Thought_Suppression_A_Meta-Analysis)
- [PK] Salkovskis PM, Reynolds M. "Thought suppression and smoking cessation." Behav Res Ther 1994;32(2):193–201, DOI [10.1016/0005-7967(94)90112-0](https://doi.org/10.1016/0005-7967(94)90112-0). Abstaining smokers instructed to suppress smoking thoughts showed increased intrusions (a rebound pattern). Details not re-verified.
- iCanQuit's comparator QuitGuide used "avoid high-risk situations / keep hands and mouth busy" strategies. iCanQuit's "accept urges and let them pass" approach outperformed it. — [Fred Hutch](https://www.fredhutch.org/en/news/releases/2020/09/fred-hutch-led-clinical-trial-shows-new-smartphone-app-helps-smokers-quit.html)

### Inferences
- **Banned or flagged UI phrases**: "Don't think about smoking", "Get it out of your head", "Fight/beat/crush the craving", "Resist", "Block the urge", "Push it away", "Stay strong and ignore it", and any "zap/delete the thought" gamification.
- **Preferred phrasing**: "Notice it", "Make room for it", "Let it be there while you…", "Ride it out", "Cravings rise and fall", "You don't have to obey it", "Thanks, mind", "That's a thought, not an order".
- **Distraction is not banned, but frame it as a choice.** Wegner's distracter finding suggests a single focused, valued activity can be helpful. Frame it as "choose something that matters to you while the urge is here", not as "get your mind off smoking". This also fits ACT committed action.
- **Onboarding demo**: a 60-second "white bear" exercise. "For 30 seconds, try *not* to think of a white bear. Tap each time it pops up." Show the count, then: "That's why this app won't ask you to push cravings away. We'll practise letting them come and go." Use a neutral target (white bear, pink elephant), never a cigarette, to avoid cue priming.
- **Do not deploy "avoid all triggers" as the primary strategy.** Avoidance-based tips can remain as secondary environmental advice (removing cigarettes from the house is still sensible), but should not be the core coping mode, given the iCanQuit vs QuitGuide result.

### Gaps
- No direct trial has tested specific *app copy* wording (suppression-framed vs acceptance-framed) on smoking outcomes. The copy recommendations are extrapolated.
- Effect sizes from thought-suppression meta-analyses were not retrieved.
- The Erskine 2010 numeric effect sizes (cigarettes per day by week) were not retrieved; only the direction was confirmed.

## Q3. Urge surfing: origin, trials (Bowen & Marlatt 2009; Brewer 2011; Craving to Quit), craving duration, and a 180-second protocol

### Takeaway
Brief urge-surfing instructions **did not reduce urge intensity but did reduce cigarettes smoked** over the following 7 days (Bowen & Marlatt 2009, n=123). The mechanism is a change in *response* to urges, not urge elimination. In-person mindfulness training beat the ALA Freedom From Smoking programme at 17 weeks (Brewer 2011). App-delivered mindfulness (Craving to Quit) improved short-term abstinence days but **not 6-month abstinence** (Garrison 2020). For the "Urge Surfer", success should therefore be defined as "rode it out / chose an action", not "urge went to zero".

### Cited Findings
- Bowen S, Marlatt GA. "Surfing the urge: brief mindfulness-based intervention for college student smokers." Psychol Addict Behav 2009;23(4):666–671:
  - Undergraduate smokers (N=123) who were interested in changing but not in treatment completed a cue-exposure paradigm, randomized to brief mindfulness instructions vs no instruction.
  - **No significant group difference in urges.** The mindfulness group **smoked significantly fewer cigarettes over 7-day follow-up**.
  - The authors suggest the technique "may not initially reduce urges to smoke but may change the response to urges".
  - — [ResearchGate abstract](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/40755906_Surfing_the_Urge_Brief_Mindfulness-Based_Intervention_for_College_Student_Smokers); [Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/journals/padbe/abstract/00011970-200912000-00013~surfing-the-urge-brief-mindfulness-based-intervention-for?redirectionsource=fulltextview); [Semantic Scholar](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Surfing-the-urge:-brief-mindfulness-based-for-Bowen-Marlatt/7bae69efb7ba32438ead7be638d8b584e18bcfd7)
  - A secondary source gives a 26% vs 11% reduction in cigarettes. **Not verified against the primary article.** — [Mindful.org](https://www.mindful.org/mindfulness-and-cravings/)
  - A later EEG study by other authors characterises the result as reduced cigarettes 7 days post-intervention vs control. — [PLOS ONE 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0191661)
- [PK] In Bowen & Marlatt's procedure, participants in the mindfulness condition handled their own cigarette pack in graded steps (look, hold, smell, put the cigarette to the lips) without smoking. They were instructed to notice sensations and thoughts without trying to change them or act on them, imagining the urge as a wave that rises, crests and subsides. Exact wording not re-verified.
- [PK] "Urge surfing" is credited to G. Alan Marlatt's relapse-prevention work. — [Marlatt & Gordon, *Relapse Prevention*, Guilford 1985; Guilford excerpt of 2nd ed.](https://www.guilford.com/excerpts/marlatt.pdf)
- Brewer JA et al. "Mindfulness training for smoking cessation: results from a randomized controlled trial." Drug Alcohol Depend 2011;119(1–2):72–80:
  - Mindfulness training (MT) vs American Lung Association Freedom From Smoking (FFS). 4-week treatment, follow-ups at 6, 12 and 17 weeks.
  - 41 participants randomized to MT, 33 of whom initiated treatment.
  - MT showed greater reduction in cigarette use and higher abstinence, maintained at follow-up.
  - A secondary source reports **31% vs 6% point-prevalence abstinence at 17 weeks (p=0.01)**.
  - — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/51461381_Mindfulness_training_for_smoking_cessation_Results_from_a_randomized_controlled_trial)
  - [PK] The abstract also reports end-of-treatment abstinence 36% vs 15% (p=.063, trend), with the 17-week difference significant (31% vs 6%, p=.012). **This reconciles the "36% vs 15%" figure in the brief. The DOI appears to be 10.1016/j.drugalcdep.2011.05.027, not 2011.03.027 as given in the brief.**
- Elwafi HM, Witkiewitz K, Mallik S, Thornhill TA, Brewer JA. "Mindfulness training for smoking cessation: moderation of the relationship between craving and cigarette use." Drug Alcohol Depend 2013. MT weakened the link between craving and smoking: the craving was still present, but less likely to translate into smoking. — [PMC3619004](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3619004); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0376871612004565)
- **Craving to Quit app** (Garrison KA et al., Nicotine Tob Res 2020, DOI 10.1093/ntr/nty126; PMID 29917096):
  - 556 adults smoking ≥5 cigarettes/day were randomized; 530 were analysed. Primary outcome: 7-day PPA at 4 weeks post-quit.
  - Weekly abstinence during the quit period ranged 21.8%–27.7% (app) vs 17.9%–19.6% (control). The app outperformed control on the proportion of abstinent days in ITT.

- [PK] Craving to Quit teaches the **RAIN** sequence (Recognize, Accept/Allow, Investigate, Note), developed in Brewer's lab.

### Inferences
**Proposed 180-second "Urge Surfer" protocol.** This is a design synthesis from Bowen & Marlatt, Brewer's RAIN, and the iCanQuit mediation finding. It has not been trial-tested as specified.

| Time | Step | User action | Narration (paraphrase-level example) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0–10 s | **Check-in** | Rate the urge 0–10 (slider). Optional one-tap context chip: stress / after meal / coffee / alcohol / boredom / people smoking | "Thanks for opening this instead of the pack. You don't have to make this urge go away — we'll just watch it together." |
| 10–30 s | **Anchor** | Two slow breaths, paced by an expanding circle (≈4 s in, 6 s out) | "Feel your feet on the floor. Let the breath set the pace." |
| 30–70 s | **Locate & label (body)** | Tap a body map: mouth, throat, chest, stomach, hands, head. Pick sensation words: tight, buzzy, hollow, warm, restless, pulling | "Where is the urge living right now? What does it feel like — not what it's telling you to do, just how it feels." |
| 70–130 s | **Ride the wave** | A wave animation tracks the breath, with the user as a surfer on the crest. Optional "thought bubble" chip ("just one") floats past | "Urges rise, peak, and fall. It may get stronger before it eases — that's normal. You're on the board, not under the water. If a thought like 'just one' shows up, notice it: that's a thought, not an order." |
| 130–160 s | **Allow & notice change** | User taps "still rising / steady / easing" | "Whatever it's doing is OK. Make a little room for it — the way you'd let a wave pass under you." |
| 160–180 s | **Re-rate & choose** | Rate 0–10 again. Pick one valued next action (water, walk, text someone, values card). The log records "Surfed ✓" | "You rode out [X] seconds of a craving without smoking. What's one thing you'll do next that matters to you?" |

Design rules derived from the evidence:
- **Success = didn't smoke / chose action, not intensity drop.** Bowen & Marlatt found no change in urge intensity but a change in behaviour. Celebrate "rode it out" even when post-rating ≥ pre-rating. Never show a "failed" state when the urge didn't fall.
- **Body-sensation emphasis.** Acceptance of physical sensations mediated iCanQuit's effect (Q1), so the core steps are body location and sensation labelling, not cognitive arguing.
- **No suppression verbs** (Q2). Use the wave metaphor rather than "beat it".
- **"Surf again" and "longer surf" buttons.** A single 3-minute window may not span the whole craving; let the user chain a second round or switch to a values or committed-action exercise.
- **Log pre/post ratings and context.** This gives the user personal data ("Your urges dropped an average of 2 points in 3 min"; "Most of your urges happen after coffee") and feeds the lapse chain analysis (Q6).
- **Expect short-term benefit.** The mindfulness app evidence shows short-term benefit with uncertain 6-month durability (Garrison 2020; Cochrane 2022). Position urge surfing as an in-the-moment tool alongside pharmacotherapy and broader ACT content, not as a standalone cure.

### Gaps
- **Craving duration ("cravings pass in 3–5 minutes")**: I found no primary EMA or laboratory source in this session. The figure is widely repeated in public-health materials, but its empirical basis is unverified. The app should say "urges rise and fall; many ease within minutes" rather than give a precise duration, unless a primary source is found.
- The Bowen & Marlatt DOI given in the brief (10.1037/a0017811) could not be verified. My recollection, unverified, is **10.1037/a0017127**. Check it.
- Exact Bowen & Marlatt instruction wording and length were not retrieved.
- No RCT has tested a ~180-s app-based urge-surfing module in isolation against an active control on biochemically verified abstinence.

## Q4. CBT / standard behavioural support (BCT taxonomy, NCSCT) vs ACT: head-to-head evidence

### Takeaway
Head-to-head evidence favours ACT in the smartphone format (iCanQuit vs guideline-based QuitGuide, OR 1.49). It is null in the web format (WebQuit vs Smokefree.gov, OR 0.91). A 2026 meta-analysis rated "psychological-theory-based apps vs traditional-behavioural apps" as **high-certainty** evidence of benefit (+69% at 3 months; +36% at 6 months). That comparison is dominated by the iCanQuit trial. Standard behavioural support has well-codified behaviour change techniques (BCTs) worth keeping as the app's scaffolding: quit date, medication advice, CO/feedback, self-monitoring and relapse planning.

### Inferences
- **Hybrid architecture**: keep the evidence-based "standard" BCT scaffold (quit date, pharmacotherapy advice and adherence prompts, self-monitoring, feedback on progress and money saved, relapse-prevention planning, ex-smoker identity). The "high certainty" label therefore rests heavily on one trial. Report it as strong single-trial-dominated evidence, not as broad replication.

- The included-trial list for Chu 2026's "psychological vs traditional" comparison was not retrieved, so the inference that it is iCanQuit-dominated is unconfirmed.
- No head-to-head trial of an ACT app vs a *full NCSCT-standard* behavioural-support app was found.
- The telephone ACT vs CBT full-scale trial results were not retrieved.

## Q5. App-efficacy meta-analyses: is the "January 2026 BMJ EBM meta-analysis" real, and what is the current evidence base?

### Takeaway
**Verified: the January 2026 BMJ EBM meta-analysis exists.** It is Chu S et al., "Efficacy of smartphone apps used alone or with traditional interventions for smoking cessation: a systematic review and meta-analysis," *BMJ Evidence-Based Medicine* 2026;31(4):242–253, DOI 10.1136/bmjebm-2025-113971, PMID 41529951, with a press release dated 13 January 2026. It included 31 RCTs (12,802 participants). Results:
- Apps vs no/minimal support: 6-month RR 2.85 (1.61–5.05), low certainty.

## Q6. Lapse vs relapse: definitions, the Abstinence Violation Effect, progression evidence, and a lapse-response protocol with streak repair

### Takeaway
A lapse is any smoking after quitting; a relapse is a return to regular smoking. Trial standards tolerate a little smoking: the Russell Standard allows ≤5 cigarettes after a 2-week grace period. Marlatt's AVE predicts that **internal, stable, global, uncontrollable attributions** ("I'm weak, I've blown it") turn a lapse into a relapse. Evidence: characterological attributions predicted worse outcomes in smokers (Curry, Marlatt & Gordon 1987), and post-lapse drops in self-efficacy predicted progression (Shiffman 2005). Smoking in the **first 2 weeks** strongly predicts failure: >80% of week-2 smokers on active patch were smoking at 6 months (Kenford 1994). The app's lapse flow should therefore be immediate, non-judgmental, attribution-reframing, and most intensive in weeks 1–2.

### Cited Findings
**Definitions**
- [PK] **Russell Standard** (West R, Hajek P, Stead L, Stapleton J. "Outcome criteria in smoking cessation trials: proposal for a common standard." Addiction 2005;100(3):299–303, DOI [10.1111/j.1360-0443.2004.00995.x](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1360-0443.2004.00995.x)):
  - sustained abstinence measured from **2 weeks after the quit date** (grace period)
  - self-report of smoking **no more than 5 cigarettes** over the whole follow-up period
  - biochemical verification at the final follow-up (6 or 12 months)
  - participants lost to follow-up counted as smokers

### Inferences
**Proposed lapse protocol ("Slip Response")**: a design synthesis, not trial-tested as specified.

1. **Trigger**: the user logs any smoking ("I smoked"), or a check-in reports cigarettes > 0. One tap: "How many?" (1 / 2–5 / more than 5 / I've been smoking regularly). Then route:
   - 1 to 5 cigarettes → **lapse pathway**
   - regular smoking for ≥7 days (Hughes definition) → **relapse/re-quit pathway**
2. **Immediate response, first screen, ≤3 sentences.** No red colours, no "streak lost" animation. Example copy (original):
   > "Thanks for logging this — that honesty is part of quitting. One cigarette is a slip, not a restart: your smoke-free days still count. Let's take 60 seconds to learn from it."
3. **Attribution reframe** (targets the AVE: move from internal/stable/global to external/temporary/specific/controllable). A two-option tap:
   > "Which feels closer? (a) 'I'm just not able to quit' (b) 'That situation was tough and I didn't have a plan for it yet.'"
   - Either answer gets the response: "(b) is almost always closer to the truth — slips usually follow specific situations, and situations can be planned for."
   - Self-efficacy repair matters because a post-lapse self-efficacy drop predicts relapse (Shiffman 2005).
4. **Chain analysis**: six quick taps, about 45 s, each with chips plus "other":
   - *When?* time / day
   - *Where?* home, car, work, bar, friend's place
   - *Who?* alone, with smokers, partner, colleagues
   - *Feeling just before?* stressed, angry, low, bored, celebrating, plus a **HALT** check (Hungry, Angry, Lonely, Tired)
   - *Other substances?* alcohol, coffee
   - *Access?* whose cigarette: bought it, bummed it, had a stash
   - *The thought that tipped it?* "just one", "I deserve it", "I've already blown it today", "nobody will know"
   - *How did it feel afterwards?* This question surfaces disconfirming experience (e.g., "not as good as I expected"), in line with Brewer-style curiosity.
5. **Plan update**: auto-generate one if-then plan from the chain, e.g. "If I'm at [bar] with [friends] and feel [the pull], then I'll [step outside for 3 breaths, open Urge Surfer, hold a drink in my smoking hand]". Prompt the user to remove access (bin the rest of the pack, ask friends not to offer). If pharmacotherapy is in use, check adherence ("Did you use your patch today?"). If not, suggest it, since medication evidence is stronger than behavioural relapse prevention (Cochrane 2019 [PK]).
6. **Recommit** (iCanQuit pattern):
   - default: "Keep my quit going from now", with no new quit date required
   - optional: "Set a new quit date" plus an offer to replay the acceptance exercises
   - Never force a restart.
7. **Streak and metrics repair**:
   - **Never reset the cumulative "smoke-free days" total.** Show it as the primary number.
   - Show "current run" as secondary.
   - Add a **"never miss twice"** prompt: "Today's goal: get back to 0." It is grounded in Lally 2010's finding that a single miss did not derail habit formation.
   - Award a "**Back on track**" badge after 24 h smoke-free following a slip, and a "Comeback" badge after 7 days.
   - Do *not* advertise the Russell 5-cigarette grace to users. Telling them "you can have 5" risks licensing a lapse. Use it only internally, for outcome reporting and for routing between the lapse and relapse pathways.
8. **Early-window intensification**: because lapses in weeks 1–2 strongly predict failure (Kenford 1994; Westman 1997):
   - any lapse in days 0–14 triggers a same-day follow-up check-in, extra Urge Surfer nudges at the logged trigger time the next day, and an offer of a quitline or coach
9. **Escalation (relapse pathway)**: when ≥7 consecutive smoking days are logged, or the user says "I'm smoking again":
   - offer "Plan your next quit" with a new date, a pharmacotherapy review, and connection to a quitline
   - keep all history and badges
   - Example copy: "Most people take several tries to quit for good; each one teaches you something." This line needs a supporting source before use; see Gaps.
10. **Lapse-log data reuse**: aggregate the chain analyses into a "Your top 3 triggers" card that feeds pre-emptive, time- or location-based nudges.

### Gaps
- **No empirical study was found on gamified streak resets causing disengagement** in health or quit apps. The recommendation to keep cumulative totals rests on AVE theory and Lally 2010, not direct trial evidence. A product A/B test is recommended.
- The exact Shiffman 1996 progression percentages and the AVE-predicts-progression effect sizes were not retrieved.
- Russell Standard and Hughes 2003 definitions are [PK] and not re-verified this session.
- Population statistics for "number of quit attempts before success" (to support "most people take several tries" copy) were not retrieved; avoid specific numbers without a source.
- The Kenford 1994 >80% figure comes from a secondary summary, not the abstract.

## Q7. Self-compassion, shame, and non-judgmental language

### Takeaway
Theory (AVE: guilt plus self-blame after a lapse) and limited evidence suggest that shame and self-criticism undermine recovery, and that self-compassion training can help self-critical smokers cut down. The evidence base is small and mostly from outside smoking. Non-judgmental, person-first, "slip/lapse" language is consistent with all of it.

### Cited Findings
- The affective component of the AVE involves guilt and self-blame after a lapse, and the cognitive component involves internal, stable, global attributions. Both are theorised to drive relapse. — [Larimer, Palmer & Marlatt 1999, PMC6760427](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6760427/)
- In smokers, lapsers who attributed the lapse to controllable causes felt **worse and more guilty**. — [Springer, DOI 10.1023/A:1021853301255](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1023/A:1021853301255)
- A dictionary-type secondary source reports that later analyses supported the self-efficacy component of the AVE more consistently than the guilt and self-blame components. Low-quality source; treat as unverified. — [becisions.com](https://becisions.com/dictionary/terms/abstinence-violation-effect)
- [PK] Kelly AC, Zuroff DC, Foa CL, Gilbert P. "Who benefits from training in self-compassionate self-regulation? A study of smoking reduction." J Soc Clin Psychol 2010;29(7):727–755, DOI [10.1521/jscp.2010.29.7.727](https://doi.org/10.1521/jscp.2010.29.7.727):
  - 3-week self-compassion training reduced daily smoking faster than baseline self-monitoring
  - the benefit was concentrated among **highly self-critical** participants and those **low in readiness to change**
  - numbers not re-verified
- [PK] Randles D, Tracy JL. "Nonverbal displays of shame predict relapse and declining health in recovering alcoholics." Clin Psychol Sci 2013;1(2):149–155, DOI [10.1177/2167702612470645](https://doi.org/10.1177/2167702612470645). Shame displays (coded from video) predicted later relapse. This is alcohol, not tobacco.

### Inferences
- **Language rules**:
  - Use "slip" or "lapse" for a single episode. Use "smoking again" rather than "relapse" or "failed" in user-facing copy.
  - Never use "cheated", "fell off the wagon", "weak", "willpower", "clean/dirty" or "ruined".
  - Use person-first and identity-forward language ("you're becoming a non-smoker", "ex-smoker identity"), consistent with the standard BCT of strengthening ex-smoker identity.
  - Frame self-talk prompts as kindness toward a friend ("What would you say to a friend who just had one cigarette?").
- **Self-compassion micro-exercise** (original wording, ~45 s), offered in the lapse flow and when a self-critical thought chip is selected:
  1. "This is a hard moment."
  2. "Slips happen to lots of people who are quitting."
  3. "May I be patient with myself while I get back on track."
  - This is a three-part structure (mindfulness, common humanity, self-kindness) adapted from Neff's self-compassion model. [PK] Neff KD 2003, Self and Identity; DOI [10.1080/15298860309032](https://doi.org/10.1080/15298860309032)
- **Self-criticism screening**: the Kelly 2010 [PK] finding suggests self-critical users benefit most. Add an optional onboarding item ("When I slip up, I'm hard on myself": agree/disagree) and route high scorers to the self-compassion content earlier.

### Gaps
- No RCT of a self-compassion module inside a smoking-cessation *app* was found (search budget exhausted).
- Direct smoking-specific evidence that shame *predicts* relapse was not found. The closest evidence is the alcohol study (Randles & Tracy 2013 [PK]) plus AVE guilt findings.
- Kelly et al. 2010 effect sizes and design details are [PK] and need verification.
