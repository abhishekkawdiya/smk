## Q1. Chemical dependence: FTND/FTCD, HSI, TTFC and e-cigarette dependence measures

### Takeaway
Use the two-item Heaviness of Smoking Index (time to first cigarette (TTFC) + cigarettes per day (CPD), scored 0–6) as the dependence core, and treat TTFC on its own as the strongest single predictor. Baker et al. 2007 found that most of the FTND's predictive power for cessation came from TTFC. The FTND is reproduced everywhere, but **no source confirms it is public domain**, and the sources disagree on cutoffs. For dual users, add an "e-cig time to first use after waking" item adapted from the Penn State e-cigarette index (PS-ECDI) structure. The PS-ECDI and the PROMIS-derived E-cigarette Dependence Scale (EDS) both require contacting their authors for permission.

### Cited Findings
**FTND (1991)**
- Citation: Heatherton TF, Kozlowski LT, Frecker RC, Fagerström KO. "The Fagerström Test for Nicotine Dependence: a revision of the Fagerström Tolerance Questionnaire." *Br J Addict* 1991 Sep;86(9):1119–27. — [PubMed 1932883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1932883/). The DOI 10.1111/j.1360-0443.1991.tb01879.x is quoted in secondary sources ([search-surfaced references](https://deepblue.lib.umich.edu/bitstream/2027.42/31860/1/0000810.pdf)), but I could not open the publisher page to confirm it.
- Development: the sample was 254 smokers. Two FTQ items, the nicotine rating item and the inhalation item, were unrelated to any biochemical measure of smoking intensity and were the main sources of the FTQ's psychometric weakness. The revision dropped them and rescored TTFC and CPD. — [PubMed 1932883](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1932883/)
- Structure: 6 items, total 0–10. The first item (TTFC) is worth up to 3 points in the original 1991 structure, as reproduced on the Irish HSE and Alberta Health Services forms. — [HSE FTND form](https://www.hse.ie/eng/about/who/tobaccocontrol/resources/fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence.pdf); [Alberta Health Services FTND](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/assets/info/trp/if-trp-tct-fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence.pdf)
- Item content, described rather than quoted: (1) time to first cigarette after waking, in 4 bands; (2) difficulty refraining where smoking is forbidden (yes/no); (3) which cigarette you would most hate to give up (first of the morning vs any other); (4) cigarettes per day (≤10 / 11–20 / 21–30 / ≥31); (5) whether you smoke more in the first hours after waking than the rest of the day (yes/no); (6) whether you smoke when so ill you are in bed most of the day (yes/no). — the HSE and Alberta forms above. Item content is consistent with [EUDA drugs library entry](https://www.euda.europa.eu/drugs-library/fagerstrom-test-nicotine-dependence_en).
- **The published cutoffs conflict:**
  - Five-band scheme (NIDA CTN form; MedEdPORTAL): 0–2 very low, 3–4 low, 5 medium, 6–7 high, 8–10 very high. — [NIDA CTN CDE library](https://ctnlibrary.org/cde/2195870/); [MedEdPORTAL 2014 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/2bbbb487ecf7491db576ea2166134dd9)
  - Alberta Health Services three-band scheme, attributed to Heatherton 1991: 7–10 highly dependent, 4–6 moderately, <4 minimally. — [AHS FTND](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/assets/info/trp/if-trp-tct-fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence.pdf)
  - Irish HSE: 8+ high, 5–7 moderate. — [HSE FTND](https://www.hse.ie/eng/about/who/tobaccocontrol/resources/fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence.pdf)
  - Some digital versions alter the scoring. For example, Blueprint sums items 2–6 only. — [Blueprint FTND help article](https://ebchelp.blueprint.ai/en/articles/9908019-fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence-ftnd)
- **Copyright/permission (no public-domain status confirmed):**
  - The University of Wisconsin ARC self-report library says it does not hold copyright over the FTND and that permission must come from the questionnaire's author. Its CC BY-SA 4.0 licence covers only its scoring code. — [UW ARC FTND page](https://arc.psych.wisc.edu/self-report/fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence-ftnd/)
  - The Alberta Health Services form credits copyright to the Regents of the University of California. This is probably the copyright on that adapted form, not on the instrument. — [AHS FTND](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/assets/info/trp/if-trp-tct-fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence.pdf)
  - A version attributed to the American College of Chest Physicians tobacco toolkit states that the form may be duplicated without restriction for patient-care or educational purposes. I could not open the hosting PDF to confirm which of these hosts carries it. — [ALA-hosted Fagerström scoring sheet](https://www.lung.org/getmedia/fe61483c-86e6-408c-94cc-ab4f522c50c6/fagerstrom-tobacco-dependency-scoring-and-use); [adiktologie.cz FTND PDF](https://www.adiktologie.cz/file/142/the-fagerstrom-test-for-nicotine-dependence-en.pdf)

**FTND renamed FTCD (2012)**
- Fagerström K. "Determinants of tobacco use and renaming the FTND to the Fagerström Test for Cigarette Dependence." *Nicotine Tob Res* 2012;14(1):75–78. A trial protocol dates it 2011, probably the online-first date. — [Pulmonary Chronicles review citing it](https://pulmonarychronicles.com/index.php/pulmonarychronicles/article/download/230/561); [NCT06179290 protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/90/NCT06179290/Prot_000.pdf)
- Rationale: cigarette dependence has determinants beyond nicotine, and the test covers cigarettes only. — [Pulmonary Chronicles](https://pulmonarychronicles.com/index.php/pulmonarychronicles/article/download/230/561)

**Heaviness of Smoking Index (HSI)**
- Citation: Heatherton TF, Kozlowski LT, Frecker RC, Rickert W, Robinson J. "Measuring the heaviness of smoking: using self-reported time to the first cigarette of the day and number of cigarettes smoked per day." *Br J Addict* 1989;84(7):791–800. Some sources give pp. 791–799. The paper proposed four categories for TTFC (≤5, 6–30, 31–60, ≥61 min) and for CPD (1–10, 11–20, 21–30, ≥31). — [University at Buffalo record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/measuring-the-heaviness-of-smoking-using-selfreported-time-to-the/); [NIDA Data Share HSI](https://datashare.nida.nih.gov/instrument/heaviness-of-smoking-index)
- Scoring: each item 0–3, total 0–6. PhenX categories are low 0–1, medium 2–4, high 5–6. — [PhenX HSI protocol 330201](https://www.phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/330201)
- AHS scoring for TTFC: 3 points within 5 min, 2 for 5–30 min, 1 for 31–60 min, 0 after 60 min. For CPD: 0 for ≤10, rising to 3 for ≥31. AHS labels 5+ "high" and 1–2 "very low", so its bands differ from PhenX. — [AHS HSI form](https://www.albertahealthservices.ca/assets/info/trp/if-trp-tct-heaviness-of-smoking-index.pdf)
- Limitation: NIDA notes the HSI may have significant floor effects among lighter smokers in non-clinical populations. — [NIDA Data Share HSI](https://datashare.nida.nih.gov/instrument/heaviness-of-smoking-index)

**TTFC as the best single item**
- Citation: Baker TB, Piper ME, McCarthy DE, Bolt DM, Smith SS, Kim S-Y, Colby S, Conti D, Giovino GA, Hatsukami D, Hyland A, Krishnan-Sarin S, Niaura R, Perkins KA, Toll BA (Transdisciplinary Tobacco Use Research Center Tobacco Dependence Phenotype Workgroup). "Time to first cigarette in the morning as an index of ability to quit smoking: implications for nicotine dependence." *Nicotine Tob Res* 2007 Nov;9(Suppl 4):S555–S570. PMC2933747. **The DOI listed is 10.1080/14622200701673480, not the 10.1080/14622200701712568 given in the brief.** — [PMC2933747](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933747); [UMN Experts record](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/time-to-first-cigarette-in-the-morning-as-an-index-of-ability-to-/)
- Design: four placebo-controlled cessation trials plus one international epidemiologic study, comparing FTND, HSI, NDSS and WISDM against cessation success. — [PMC2933747](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933747)
- Findings: most of the FTND's predictive power came from TTFC, which outperformed every other single measure. The authors interpret TTFC as tapping "heavy, uninterrupted, and automatic smoking" and propose it as a good single-item dependence measure. — [PMC2933747](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933747)

**E-cigarette dependence (dual users)**
- PS-ECDI citation: Foulds J, Veldheer S, Yingst J, Hrabovsky S, Wilson SJ, Nichols TT, Eissenberg T. "Development of a questionnaire for assessing dependence on electronic cigarettes among a large sample of ex-smoking e-cigarette users." *Nicotine Tob Res* 2015;17(2):186–192. doi:10.1093/ntr/ntu204. PMC4838001. — [PMC4838001](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4838001)
- PS-ECDI structure: 10 items covering frequency of use, first use after waking, waking at night to use, craving, withdrawal and difficulty abstaining. Total 0–20. Bands: 0–3 not dependent, 4–8 low, 9–12 medium, ≥13 high. Penn State gives these bands for the cigarette index (PSCDI), from which the e-cig version was adapted. Blueprint lists the same bands for both. — [Penn State Nicotine Dependence Index page](https://med.psu.edu/departments-faculty/center-for-research-on-tobacco-and-health/nicotine-dependence-index); [Blueprint PSCDI/PSECDI](https://help.blueprint.ai/en/articles/12492486-penn-state-electronic-cigarette-dependence-index-pscdi)
- In a trial-protocol reproduction, the first-use-after-waking item scores from 5 points (quickest) down to 0, and the top daily-use band is ≥30 uses per day. Craving items score 0–2 and yes/no items 0–1. — [NCT04429932 protocol](https://cdn.clinicaltrials.gov/large-docs/32/NCT04429932/Prot_000.pdf); [Penn State page](https://med.psu.edu/departments-faculty/center-for-research-on-tobacco-and-health/nicotine-dependence-index)
- Permission: the Penn State questionnaire PDF lists Dr Foulds' contact details for use. — [Maine AAP copy of PS-ECDI](https://www.maineaap.org/assets/conferences/Penn-State-Electronic-Cigarette-Dependence-Index.pdf). NCSCT (UK) summarises vaping dependence assessments. — [NCSCT vaping dependence assessments](https://www.ncsct.co.uk/library/view/pdf/Assessments-for-nicotine-vaping-dependence.pdf)
- PROMIS-E (4 items, adolescents): Morean ME et al. "Assessing nicotine dependence in adolescent e-cigarette users: the 4-item PROMIS Nicotine Dependence Item Bank for electronic cigarettes." *Drug Alcohol Depend* 2018;188:60–63. N = 520 adolescents; single-factor fit RMSEA 0.02, CFI 0.99, SRMR 0.01. — [CORESTA CROM: 4-item EDS](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/4-item-e-cigarette-dependence-scale)
- EDS (adults): adapted from the PROMIS smoking item bank and tested as 22-, 8- and 4-item versions in 1,009 adult e-cigarette users. All versions were internally consistent, and the longer forms added little over the 4-item version. Listed DOI 10.1093/ntr/ntx271; sources date it 2018 or 2019. CROM says to contact the author for use permission. — [CORESTA CROM: EDS](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/e-cigarette-dependence-scale); [Penn State Pure record](https://pure.psu.edu/en/publications/psychometric-evaluation-of-the-e-cigarette-dependence-scale/)

### Inferences
- Copyright protects expression (wording and layout), not facts. TTFC and CPD are factual questions with standard numeric bands. An app can therefore ask "time to first cigarette" and "cigarettes per day" in its own words, use the 4×4 HSI categories, and cite Heatherton 1989/1991, without reproducing FTND text. Have counsel confirm this before commercial launch.
- Use HSI 0–6 with the PhenX bands (0–1 low, 2–4 moderate, 5–6 high) as the canonical dependence tier, because those bands are the most widely used and come from a registry. Add a "TTFC ≤5 min" override flag that forces at least the "moderate-high" tier, following Baker 2007.
- The five-band FTND scheme in the brief (0–2 / 3–4 / 5 / 6–7 / 8–10) matches the NIDA CTN form, but the HSE and AHS forms use different bands. The blueprint should name the scheme it adopts and not call it "the" FTND cutoff.
- For dual users, one item (time to first vape after waking, same bands) gives a parallel e-cig signal at almost no time cost. A full PS-ECDI or EDS can be offered later in the app as an optional deep-dive once permission is obtained.

### Gaps
- FTND internal consistency (the commonly quoted α ≈ 0.61) and the FTND–cotinine correlations from the 1991 paper could not be confirmed. Search returned only qualitative statements.
- I found no definitive statement from Fagerström or the publisher putting the FTND/FTCD in the public domain. The commercial-use position is unresolved.
- DOI for Fagerström 2012 (FTCD rename) not confirmed. It may be 10.1093/ntr/ntr137, which is unverified training knowledge.
- Exact PS-ECDI e-cig item scoring and the paper's validation statistics (reliability, comparison with cigarette dependence) were not retrieved.
- Whether PROMIS-derived items are free under HealthMeasures/PROMIS terms was not checked. CROM says to contact the author.

## Q2. WISDM-68 and Brief WISDM-37: subscales, PDM/SDM, relapse prediction, licensing

### Takeaway
WISDM gives a theory-based 13-motive map (68 items), and the Brief WISDM (37 items, 11 subscales) splits into Primary Dependence Motives (PDM: automaticity, loss of control, craving, tolerance) and Secondary Dependence Motives (SDM). Both are far too long for a <90 s onboarding, and CROM says they require author permission. The app should borrow the construct taxonomy only and use its own items.

### Cited Findings
- WISDM-68 citation: Piper ME, Piasecki TM, Federman EB, Bolt DM, Smith SS, Fiore MC, Baker TB. "A multiple motives approach to tobacco dependence: the Wisconsin Inventory of Smoking Dependence Motives (WISDM-68)." *J Consult Clin Psychol* 2004;72(2):139–154. doi:10.1037/0022-006X.72.2.139. — [CORESTA CROM: WISDM](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/wisconsin-inventory-smoking-dependence-motives)
- Validation: 68 items on 13 theoretically derived motives. In N = 775 smokers, all 13 subscales had acceptable internal consistency, varied with smoking heaviness and showed a multidimensional structure. Validity analyses linked subscales to smoking heaviness, DSM-IV dependence and relapse symptoms. — [CORESTA CROM: WISDM](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/wisconsin-inventory-smoking-dependence-motives); [NIDA Data Share WISDM](https://datashare.nida.nih.gov/instrument/wisconsin-index-smoking-dependence-motives)
- 13 subscales: affiliative attachment, automaticity, behavioral choice/melioration, cognitive enhancement, craving, cue exposure/associative processes, loss of control, negative reinforcement, positive reinforcement, social/environmental goads, taste/sensory properties, tolerance, weight control. — [CORESTA CROM: WISDM](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/wisconsin-inventory-smoking-dependence-motives)
- Relapse: press coverage of the 2004 paper said automatic smoking, smoking to relieve stress, smoking to enhance mental activity, and being in a smoking environment were the motives most connected to relapse. This is a secondary press source. — [HealthDay "Why light up?"](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/mental-health/why-light-up-519158.html)
- Licensing: CROM states that use of the WISDM requires permission from the author. — [CORESTA CROM: WISDM](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/wisconsin-inventory-smoking-dependence-motives)
- **Brief WISDM-37** (Smith SS, Piper ME, Bolt DM, Fiore MC, Wetter DW, Cinciripini PM, Baker TB. *Nicotine Tob Res* 2010; DOI given in brief as 10.1093/ntr/ntq077, not independently confirmed):
  - 37 items, 11 subscales. Behavioral choice/melioration is dropped, and negative plus positive reinforcement are merged into "affective enhancement". — [CORESTA CROM: Brief WISDM](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/brief-version-wisconsin-inventory-smoking-dependence-motives)
  - PDM = automaticity, loss of control, craving, tolerance, with 4 items each. The other subscales are SDM with 3 items each. All items use a 7-point Likert scale. — [CORESTA CROM: Brief WISDM](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/brief-version-wisconsin-inventory-smoking-dependence-motives); scoring sheet hosted at [UW-CTRI Brief WISDM questionnaire and scoring PDF](https://ctri.wisc.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/217/2019/04/Brief-WISDM-Questionnaire-and-Scoring.pdf), which I could not open.
  - Reported psychometrics: adequate internal consistency, good convergent validity and predictive validity. In the summaries I found, PDM added prediction beyond the FTND for nicotine self-administration and current urge; relapse was not mentioned. — [CORESTA CROM: Brief WISDM](https://www.coresta.org/crom/psychometric/brief-version-wisconsin-inventory-smoking-dependence-motives)
- Replication: a Hungarian student sample fit the theoretical 13-factor structure less well than expected, and the authors fell back on exploratory factor analysis. — [REAL (MTAK) repository](https://real.mtak.hu/75513/)

### Inferences
- Arithmetic check: 11 subscales − 4 PDM = 7 SDM. 4×4 + 7×3 = 16 + 21 = 37 items, which matches the 37-item total. One search summary said "six" SDM subscales, which is inconsistent with the item count.
- The four app phenotypes map onto WISDM constructs: **affect regulation** to negative reinforcement / affective enhancement; **cue-conditioned** to cue exposure/associative processes; **automatic/boredom** to automaticity, which is also a PDM and tied to TTFC in Baker 2007; **social** to social/environmental goads and affiliative attachment. Only automaticity (and craving) sit within PDM, so "automatic" and "high HSI" will co-occur. The scoring rubric should not count the same signal twice.
- Because the app items are original single items per construct, they are not WISDM. The app should not claim WISDM validity for them, only "constructs informed by WISDM".

### Gaps
- Brief WISDM item-to-subscale mapping, reverse-scored items and the exact PDM/SDM computation (mean of subscale means?) were not retrieved because the CTRI PDF could not be fetched.
- Published PDM-vs-SDM relapse effect sizes (e.g., Piper et al. 2008 *J Abnorm Psychol* on refining the dependence phenotype) were not retrieved before the search budget ran out.
- Whether UW-CTRI grants permission for commercial apps, and on what terms, was not found.

## Q3. Horn–Waingrow / Reasons for Smoking and the NIH "Why Do You Smoke?" self-test

### Takeaway
The Horn self-test, distributed by NIH/NCI as "Why do you smoke?", scores six factors with 3 items each: stimulation, handling, pleasurable relaxation, crutch/tension reduction, craving/psychological addiction, and habit. A factor score of 11+ is "high" and 7 or under is "low". It is a U.S. Public Health Service / NIH publication, which makes public-domain status likely but not confirmed. I found no predictive validity data for it.

### Cited Findings
- An NCI-hosted PDF titled "Why do you smoke?" carries U.S. Public Health Service / NIH branding. It describes itself as a short self-test that helps smokers understand their reasons for smoking and choose a quitting approach. — [NCI EBCCP-hosted "Why do you smoke?" PDF](https://ebccp.cancercontrol.cancer.gov/uploads/RTIPS/WHE/DoHHS/NIH/NCI/DCCPS/2291.pdf;jsessionid=BF637A7FFA66C3C275F0553424D95B62)
- Six factors: three positive-feeling factors (stimulation, handling, pleasurable relaxation), plus reducing negative feelings (tension, anxiety, anger, shame), craving as psychological addiction, and habit smoking. — [NCI-hosted PDF](https://ebccp.cancercontrol.cancer.gov/uploads/RTIPS/WHE/DoHHS/NIH/NCI/DCCPS/2291.pdf;jsessionid=BF637A7FFA66C3C275F0553424D95B62)
- Scoring (UCLA version, credited to the "Horn Smoker's Self Test"): sum 3 items per factor; 11+ is high and 7 or under is low. — [UCLA Health "Why do you smoke" PDF](https://www.uclahealth.org/smokefree/Workfiles/Why-do-you-smoke.pdf). Nova Scotia 811 also treats 11+ on any set as a strong influence. — [Nova Scotia 811 quiz](https://811.novascotia.ca/health_topics/tobacco-addiction-why-do-i-smoke-quiz/). A U.S. Coast Guard adaptation also exists. — [USCG Alameda "Why You Smoke"](https://dcms.uscg.mil/Portals/10/DOL/Base%20Alameda/Alameda-HSWL-Why_You_Smoke.pdf)
- The search found no data on the self-test's ability to predict quitting. — [NCI-hosted PDF](https://ebccp.cancercontrol.cancer.gov/uploads/RTIPS/WHE/DoHHS/NIH/NCI/DCCPS/2291.pdf;jsessionid=BF637A7FFA66C3C275F0553424D95B62)

### Inferences
- If items are rated 1–5 (implied by a 3-item sum with an 11+ threshold, range 3–15), then 11+ means a mean of about 3.7 per item, between "occasionally" and "frequently". I have not confirmed the response anchors.
- Mapping Horn factors to app phenotypes: crutch/tension reduction → affect regulation; habit → automatic/boredom; handling, stimulation and pleasurable relaxation → partly cue/ritual and positive reinforcement; craving → dependence (overlaps HSI). Horn has **no social factor**, so the social phenotype has to come from WISDM (social/environmental goads) or Velicer (positive affect/social situations).
- Works of the U.S. federal government are generally not copyrightable under 17 U.S.C. §105. That makes the NIH/NCI booklet probably public domain, but this is a legal inference without a cited source. It also may not cover the underlying Horn/Ikard item text if that was first published privately. Paraphrase anyway.

### Gaps
- Original citations not retrieved: Ikard FF, Green DE, Horn D (1969, *Int J Addict*) factor analysis of reasons for smoking; Russell MAH, Peto J, Patel UA (1974, *J R Stat Soc A*) typology; Horn & Waingrow (1966) original.
- No psychometrics (reliability, factor replication, predictive validity) found for the Horn self-test.
- I found no explicit public-domain statement on the NCI PDF.

## Q4. Other brief motive, trigger and craving instruments (Velicer self-efficacy/temptation, QSU-Brief, Shiffman situational triggers)

### Takeaway
Velicer et al.'s 1990 Smoking Self-Efficacy/Temptation scale gives a validated three-situation taxonomy: positive affect/social, negative affect, and habitual/craving. It maps cleanly onto the app's phenotypes and supplies the missing "social" axis. The QSU-Brief (10 items, 2 factors) measures state craving, not trait phenotype, so it belongs in in-app check-ins rather than onboarding. EMA data show negative affect rising in the hours before stress-attributed lapses.

### Cited Findings
- Velicer WF, DiClemente CC, Rossi JS, Prochaska JO. "Relapse situations and self-efficacy: an integrative model." *Addict Behav* 1990;15:271–283. — [URI Cancer Prevention Research Center, long form](https://web.uri.edu/cprc/measures/smoking/self-efficacy-temptation-long-form/)
- Long-form subscale keys (20 items): positive affect/social = items 1, 4, 6, 9, 10, 16; negative affect = items 3, 5, 7, 8, 18, 19; habitual/craving = items 11, 13, 14, 15, 20. The URI page prints the last key as "11, 131 14, 15, 20", an evident typo. Habitual/craving includes situations such as first waking up and craving a cigarette. — [URI CPRC long form](https://web.uri.edu/cprc/measures/smoking/self-efficacy-temptation-long-form/). A short form (9 items) is also hosted. — [URI CPRC short form](https://web.uri.edu/cprc/measures/smoking/self-efficacy-temptation-short-form/); scoring at [UMBC Habits Lab](https://habitslab.umbc.edu/self-efficacy-scales-scoring)
- In 957 volunteers across five stages of change, the scale was reported as "extremely reliable and coherent", with identifiable subcomponents. — [URI CPRC long form](https://web.uri.edu/cprc/measures/smoking/self-efficacy-temptation-long-form/)
- QSU-Brief (Cox LS, Tiffany ST, Christen AG, 2001, *Nicotine Tob Res*): 10 items; tested in 221 laboratory smokers and 112 smokers entering a cessation program. Factor 1 is desire/intention to smoke (smoking as rewarding); Factor 2 is anticipated relief from negative affect with urgent desire. High internal consistency was reported across settings. — [University at Buffalo record](https://researchconnect.buffalo.edu/en/publications/evaluation-of-the-brief-questionnaire-of-smoking-urges-qsu-brief-/)
- QSU-Brief cross-cultural results: Dutch N = 208, α > 0.83, factor structure slightly different. — [Erasmus repository](https://repub.eur.nl/pub/22715). Bengali N = 460, α = 0.94. — [APJCP Bengali QSU-Brief](https://journal.waocp.org/article_89887.html)
- Negative affect and lapses (prospective EMA, N = 215): day-to-day changes in stress and negative affect did not predict next-day lapse. Within the lapse day, negative affect was already rising hours before lapses, but only for episodes attributed to stress or bad mood. — [USUHS scholar record "Negative affect and smoking lapses: a prospective analysis"](https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/negative-affect-and-smoking-lapses-a-prospective-analysis/)
- In within-day pattern work, only the negative-affect pattern predicted first lapse, and only in EMA assessments, not questionnaire measures. — [RAND external publication EP20110026](https://www.rand.org/pubs/external_publications/EP20110026.html)

### Inferences
- Velicer's three situation types and WISDM support splitting "social" from "positive affect" and treating "habitual/craving" as close to automatic smoking. That supports the app's 4-phenotype scheme (affect, cue, automatic, social) as a reasonable construct-level simplification. The scheme itself is not validated.
- Because negative affect predicts lapses only in momentary data, the onboarding trait item for affect regulation should be treated as a prior. It should be updated by in-app EMA-style check-ins, for example a 2-item urge/mood check modelled on the two QSU-Brief factors in original wording.

### Gaps
- Velicer 1990 DOI not confirmed. The brief's partial DOI "10.1016/0306-4603(90)90036-?" was not verified, and I could not retrieve a reliable alternative.
- Exact QSU-Brief citation details (volume/pages/DOI; commonly 2001;3(1):7–16) and licensing were not retrieved.
- Shiffman's situational-trigger taxonomy and the 1996 *J Consult Clin Psychol* first-lapse paper were not retrieved. The USUHS record above is the closest match. Its authors and year (likely Shiffman & Waters 2004, *JCCP*) are unconfirmed.
- Licensing terms for the URI CPRC measures were not retrieved.

## Q5. Readiness: TTM staging algorithm, criticisms, Motivation To Stop Scale, Contemplation Ladder

### Takeaway
The DiClemente et al. 1991 staging algorithm is fast and well understood: preparation requires intending to quit within 30 days **plus** a 24-hour quit attempt in the past year. However, stage-matched interventions are no better than non-staged ones in Cochrane analyses. Cochrane also warns against limiting quit advice to "prepared" smokers. The single-item Motivation To Stop Scale (MTSS) predicts quit attempts (OR ≈ 6.8 top vs bottom level; AUC 0.67). Readiness should therefore modulate tone and the default CTA. It should never gate access to quit tools.

### Inferences
- The best fit for a 1–2 tap budget is one original "where are you with quitting" item that maps to TTM stages (for continuity with the TTM literature and the stakeholder vocabulary), plus a conditional past-year 24-h quit-attempt item shown only to "within 30 days" respondents. MTSS-style desire wording is attractive given its predictive data, but its licensing is unknown and the 7 anchors are distinctive text. The app should use original wording.
- Given the Cochrane finding, precontemplators should get motivational content and a default "cut down / explore" path. They should still see the quit-plan option, not be locked out of it.

## Q6. EPPM threat vs efficacy: Witte 1992, Risk Behavior Diagnosis scale, meta-analyses, defensive avoidance, health anxiety

### Takeaway
In the EPPM, threat (severity + susceptibility) sets how intense the response is, and efficacy (response efficacy + self-efficacy) sets its direction. The Risk Behavior Diagnosis (RBD) scale operationalises this as 12 items, 3 per construct. Its critical value = efficacy sum − threat sum: positive means danger control (process the message), negative means fear control (defensive avoidance, so lead with efficacy). Meta-analyses support strong-threat **plus** strong-efficacy messaging (Witte & Allen 2000). Tannenbaum et al. 2015 found d ≈ 0.29 and larger effects when efficacy statements are included. Whether fear appeals backfire in low-efficacy audiences is still disputed.

### Cited Findings
- Witte K. "Putting the fear back into fear appeals: the extended parallel process model." *Commun Monogr* 1992;59(4):329–349. DOI 10.1080/03637759209376276, as listed on Wikipedia, not publisher-verified.
  - The paper explains why fear appeals fail, re-incorporates fear as a central variable, and specifies the threat × efficacy relationship in propositions.
  - Threat determines response intensity and efficacy determines response type. Fear leads to message rejection, while threat and efficacy perceptions lead to acceptance.
  — [Wikipedia EPPM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_parallel_process_model); [SWOV record](https://swov.nl/en/publicatie/putting-fear-back-fear-appeals-extended-parallel-process-model)
- RBD scale: Witte K (with Cameron KA, McKeon JK, Berkowitz JM per the brief). "Predicting risk behaviors: development and validation of a diagnostic scale." *J Health Commun* 1996;1(4):317–342. doi:10.1080/108107396127988. Developed with HIV/AIDS prevention in mind, to diagnose which message would work best for an audience. — [UCL constructDB entry](https://verdi.cs.ucl.ac.uk/constructDB/publications/witte-predicting-1996.html)
- RBD structure: a COVID-19 adaptation used 12 RBD-based items (self-efficacy items 1–3, response efficacy 4–6, susceptibility 7–9, severity 10–12) rated 1–7 for agreement, plus a behavioural-intention item. — [PMC7643556 Table 1](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7643556/table/table-1)
- Factor structure: a French diabetes adaptation found a stable 3-factor structure (total efficacy, severity, susceptibility). Response efficacy and self-efficacy collapsed into a single efficacy factor. — [HAL 01988362](https://hal.parisnanterre.fr/hal-01988362v1)
- Critical value: sum threat items and efficacy items separately, then subtract threat from efficacy. A positive value means danger control and a negative value means fear control, so messages should focus on efficacy. — [University of Pittsburgh lecture notes](https://sites.pitt.edu/~super1/lecture/lec9151/013.htm)
- Smoking application: a study in Australian Aboriginal smokers measured perceived threat (susceptibility, severity) and perceived efficacy (response efficacy, self-efficacy for quitting) with the RBD.
  - High efficacy with high threat was consistent with danger-control dominance. Low efficacy with high threat was consistent with fear-control dominance.
  - Reported α = 0.65–1.0. The upper bound of 1.0 is implausible and probably reflects a 2-item subscale or a reporting artefact.
  — [Preventive Medicine Reports 2015 via DOAJ](https://doaj.org/article/1a078cfde9294e61807b05a1f1385924)
- Threat × efficacy quadrant typologies (control-response types with attitudinal descriptions) are used in recent applied work. — [BMC Public Health 2024 Table 1](https://bmcpublichealth.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12889-024-18164-y/tables/1)
- Witte K, Allen M. "A meta-analysis of fear appeals: implications for effective public health campaigns." *Health Educ Behav* 2000;27(5):591–615. doi:10.1177/109019810002700506.
  - Strong fear appeals raise perceived severity and susceptibility and persuade more than weak ones.
  - Strong fear plus high efficacy produced the most behaviour change. Strong fear plus low efficacy produced the most defensive responses, such as avoidance and reactance.
  — [PubMed 11009129](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11009129/); [CiteDrive DOI record](https://www.citedrive.com/en/discovery/a-meta-analysis-of-fear-appeals-implications-for-effective-public-health-campaigns)
- Tannenbaum MB, Hepler J, Zimmerman RS, Saul L, Jacobs S, Wilson K, Albarracín D. "Appealing to fear: a meta-analysis of fear appeal effectiveness and theories." *Psychol Bull* 2015;141(6):1178–1204. doi:10.1037/a0039729.
  - Sample: 127 articles (9% unpublished), 248 independent samples, N = 27,372.
  - Overall effect: d = 0.29 (random effects) on attitudes, intentions and behaviours.
  - Moderators: effects were larger when messages included efficacy statements, with high depicted susceptibility and severity, for one-time behaviours, and with more female audiences.
  - The authors reported no conditions under which fear appeals backfired.
  — [PubMed 26501228](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26501228/); [APS summary](https://www.psychologicalscience.org/video/do-scare-tactics-work-a-meta-analytic-test-of-fear-appeal-theories.html)
- Dissent: Kok and colleagues (2018) argued that defensive reactions can occur in people with low self-efficacy, and the Tannenbaum group replied. — [PMC6190830](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6190830). Tannenbaum et al. also note that prior meta-analyses found positive (Witte & Allen 2000), null (de Hoog et al. 2007) or negative (Peters, Ruiter & Kok 2013) effects. — [Tannenbaum et al. full text hosted at UPenn ASC](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2021-07/e3eff4_f9429b1d5e644365afa9ba68ba35298c.pdf)
- Bigsby & Albarracín's meta-analysis: fear appeals influenced behaviour more when they included positive **response-efficacy** information. Effects did not vary with self-efficacy information. — [Bigsby & Albarracín, UPenn ASC PDF](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2022-06/Bigsby%20and%20Albarracin.pdf)
- Health-anxiety screening: on the 14-item Whiteley Index, the optimal cutoff for severe health anxiety (DSM-IV hypochondriasis) was 5, with sensitivity and specificity of 92.6–99.4%. N = 347: 158 severe health anxiety, 97 OCD, 92 healthy controls. Hedman et al. 2015, *PLOS ONE*. — [PMC4388630](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4388630/)

### Inferences
- With one item per construct on a 1–7 scale, threat T = SEV + SUS (2–14), efficacy E = RE + SE (2–14), and critical value CV = E − T (−12 to +12). Two items per side are the minimum that preserves the EPPM's two-component logic. Single items cannot yield α, so reliability has to come from test-retest or in-app re-measurement.
- The Bigsby & Albarracín result suggests the efficacy-first copy should emphasise "this works" (response efficacy: e.g., combination NRT roughly 25% more effective than single NRT, see Q7). Self-efficacy pep talk alone may not be enough.
- Defensive avoidance can be detected with (a) the CV sign, (b) one original agree/disagree item about preferring not to think about smoking's health effects, and (c) behaviour: skipping or rapidly dismissing health-risk cards, opting out of health notifications. Users with negative CV or a DA flag should get no graphic or loss-framed imagery until E rises.
- A full Whiteley/SHAI screen does not fit the 90 s budget and could be stigmatising. Safer options: a soft opt-out ("Prefer not to see health-risk content?") and capping threat intensity for users with high SEV and SUS but low E.

### Gaps
- Original RBD item wording, response format (5- vs 7-point in the 1996 original), and any validated cutoffs for "high" vs "low" threat or efficacy were not retrieved. RBD licensing was not found.
- Peters, Ruiter & Kok 2013 (*Health Psychol Rev*) and the Kok et al. 2018 critique were not retrieved in detail.
- No validated 6- or 7-item Whiteley short form was found.
- No smoking-specific RBD psychometrics beyond the Aboriginal sample were retrieved, e.g. graphic warning label studies that used RBD.

## Q7. Design: an under-90-second item set, scoring rubric, classification rules, phenotype → intervention mapping, and evidence for tailoring

### Takeaway
A 12-item core (≤15 with conditional and optional items) can classify dependence (HSI), trigger phenotype (4 original items), readiness (TTM-mapped plus a conditional 24-h attempt item) and EPPM quadrant (4 items). The evidence base supports **modest** expectations for tailoring. Tailored print shows r = .074 (Noar 2007). In Cochrane, tailored vs non-tailored print with matched contact gives RR 1.07 (n.s.), while tailored material with more contact gives RR 1.42. Benefits therefore come from tailoring plus continued, interactive contact, not from a one-off classification. High dependence should push the dashboard toward combination NRT (RR 1.25 vs single NRT, high-certainty evidence).

### Cited Findings
- Noar SM, Benac CN, Harris MS. "Does tailoring matter? Meta-analytic review of tailored print health behavior change interventions." *Psychol Bull* 2007;133(4):673–693. doi:10.1037/0033-2909.133.4.673.
  - 57 studies, cumulative N = 58,454; weighted mean r = .074.
  - Significant moderators: comparison condition, behaviour, population, material type, number of contacts, follow-up length, and the number and type of theoretical concepts tailored on.
  — [PubMed 17592961](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17592961/)
- Lustria MLA et al. "A meta-analysis of web-delivered tailored health behavior change interventions." *J Health Commun* 2013;18(9):1039–1069. 40 studies, N = 20,180. An erratum appeared in 18(11). — [Scinapse record](https://scinapse.io/papers/2057902960); [UMSL erratum record](https://profiles.umsl.edu/en/publications/erratum-a-meta-analysis-of-web-delivered-tailored-health-behavior/)
- Livingstone-Banks J, Ordóñez-Mena JM, Hartmann-Boyce J. "Print-based self-help interventions for smoking cessation." *Cochrane Database Syst Rev* 2019;CD001118.pub4 (moderate-certainty evidence):
  - Tailored vs standard or stage-matched materials, contact matched: 10 studies, n = 11,024, RR 1.07 (95% CI 0.89–1.30).
  - Tailored multiple mailings vs a single standard mailing: 9 studies, n = 14,166, RR 1.42 (1.20–1.68).
  — [Cochrane CD001118.pub4](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD001118.pub4/information); [PMC7112723 table](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7112723/table/CD001118-tbl-0005)
- Mobile interventions: Whittaker R, McRobbie H, Bullen C, Rodgers A, Gu Y, Dobson R. "Mobile phone text messaging and app-based interventions for smoking cessation." *Cochrane* 2019;CD006611.pub5. The U.S. Community Preventive Services Task Force summary of this review found that effective interventions were tailored, interactive or both. — [Auckland ResearchSpace record](https://researchspace.auckland.ac.nz/handle/2292/49798?show=full); [The Community Guide TFFRS](https://www.thecommunityguide.org/content/tffrs-tobacco-use-mobile-phone-based-cessation-interventions)
- Smoke Free app (Crane et al., *F1000Research*): exploratory, not pre-registered. 28,112 adult smokers who set a quit date were randomised to the full app (daily "missions" with push notifications for preventing and managing cravings, plus badges and progress) or a reduced app without missions. — [F1000Research 7:1524](https://f1000research.com/articles/7-1524/v1/xml)
- A pragmatic Smoke Free RCT (Jackson et al.; protocol in *Addiction* 2019, planned n = 4,990, primary outcome 26-week continuous abstinence) was later reported as a preprint. — [PMC6347038 protocol](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6347038); [medRxiv 2023.01.12.23284463](https://medrxiv.org/content/10.1101/2023.01.12.23284463.full.pdf)
- Combination NRT: Lindson N et al. "Different doses, durations and modes of delivery of nicotine replacement therapy for smoking cessation." *Cochrane* 2019;CD013308.
  - Combination NRT (fast-acting plus patch) vs single form: RR 1.25 (95% CI 1.15–1.36), 14 studies, n = 11,356, high certainty.
  - 21 mg patches beat 14 mg patches.
  - Pre-quit NRT had moderate-certainty benefit.
  - An updated version (CD013308.pub2, searches to April 2022, 68 studies) exists, so check whether the estimate changed.
  — [Cochrane CD013308](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013308/information); [NIHR Evidence alert](https://evidence.nihr.ac.uk/alert/using-both-nicotine-patches-and-gum-together-improves-the-chances-of-quitting-smoking/); [Cochrane CD013308.pub2](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD013308.pub2)
- Supporting evidence for specific routing rules:
  - Stage-matching adds no proven benefit, and advice should not be restricted by stage. — [Cochrane CD004492.pub4](https://cochranelibrary.com/cdsr/doi/10.1002/14651858.CD004492.pub4/information)
  - Negative affect rises hours before stress-attributed lapses. — [USUHS record](https://scholar.usuhs.edu/en/publications/negative-affect-and-smoking-lapses-a-prospective-analysis/)
  - TTFC indexes automatic, heavy smoking. — [PMC2933747](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2933747)
  - Fear appeals work best with response efficacy. — [PubMed 11009129](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11009129/); [Bigsby & Albarracín](https://www.asc.upenn.edu/sites/default/files/2022-06/Bigsby%20and%20Albarracin.pdf)

### Inferences
**These items, thresholds and rules are my proposed design. None of it is validated. All item wording is original.**

**A. Item set: 12 core taps, plus up to 2 conditional taps and 1 optional tap (single-choice, one item per screen, auto-advance)**

| # | Domain | Original item (proposed wording) | Options → points |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Product | "What do you use right now?" | Cigarettes / Vapes / Both / Other tobacco. Routes items 2–4. |
| 2 | Dependence (TTFC) | "After you wake up, how soon do you usually have your first cigarette?" | ≤5 min = 3 · 6–30 min = 2 · 31–60 min = 1 · >60 min = 0 (HSI bands) |
| 3 | Dependence (CPD) | "On a typical day, about how many cigarettes do you smoke?" | ≤10 = 0 · 11–20 = 1 · 21–30 = 2 · ≥31 = 3 (HSI bands) |
| 3b | Conditional, vape/both | "After you wake up, how soon do you usually first vape?" | Same 4 time bands, 3–0 |
| 4 | Trigger: AFFECT | "I reach for a cigarette to take the edge off when I'm stressed, anxious, angry or down." | Never 0 · Rarely 1 · Sometimes 2 · Often 3 · Almost always 4 |
| 5 | Trigger: CUE | "Certain places, times or routines (coffee, after meals, driving, breaks) switch on an urge to smoke." | 0–4, same anchors |
| 6 | Trigger: AUTOMATIC | "I often light up without really deciding to, out of habit or boredom." | 0–4 |
| 7 | Trigger: SOCIAL | "I smoke most when I'm around other smokers, or out drinking or socialising." | 0–4 |
| 8 | Readiness | "Where are you with quitting right now?" | Not planning to in the next 6 months (PC) · Thinking about it in the next 6 months (C) · Planning to quit in the next 30 days (→ 8b) · Already quit, under 6 months (A) · Quit 6+ months, staying quit (M) · "I want to cut down first" (reduction pathway, treated as PC/C for tone) |
| 8b | Conditional | "In the past year, have you gone at least 24 hours without smoking because you were trying to quit?" | Yes → Preparation · No → Contemplation (DiClemente algorithm) |
| 9 | EPPM severity | "If I keep smoking, the damage to my health could be serious." | 1–7, strongly disagree → strongly agree |
| 10 | EPPM susceptibility | "I'm likely to get a smoking-related illness if I don't stop." | 1–7 |
| 11 | EPPM response efficacy | "Stopping smoking would really lower my health risks." | 1–7 |
| 12 | EPPM self-efficacy | "I'm confident I can quit, with the right support." | 1–7 |
| 13 | Optional DA probe | "Honestly, I'd rather not think about what smoking does to my health." | 1–7 |

Timing: ~12–15 single taps. The 90 s target is plausible only if per-item time is ≲6 s, which has not been verified (see Q8). Ship with paradata timing and cut item 13 or 3b first if the median exceeds 90 s.

**B. Scoring and classification**
- **Dependence tier.**
  - HSI = item 2 + item 3 (0–6): Low 0–1, Moderate 2–4, High 5–6 (PhenX bands).
  - Override: TTFC ≤5 min sets the tier to at least "Moderate-High" (Baker 2007).
  - Dual users: if item 3b ≥2 (first vape ≤30 min), raise the tier one step, capped at High. This is a heuristic.
  - Vape-only users: tier from item 3b alone, ≥2 = Moderate+, until a validated e-cig measure is licensed.
- **Trigger phenotype.**
  - Primary = argmax(AFF, CUE, AUTO, SOC).
  - Tie-break priority: AFF > AUTO > CUE > SOC. Rationale: negative affect precedes stress-attributed lapses; press coverage of WISDM-68 tied automaticity, stress relief and smoking environment to relapse, with no social signal; social is the most situational and easiest to schedule around.
  - Secondary = second-highest score if it is ≥3 ("Often").
  - "Diffuse" = max − min ≤1 and max ≥3. Use a balanced dashboard.
  - "Low-trigger" = all ≤1. The dashboard is dependence-led (withdrawal and NRT focus).
  - Store raw scores so EMA check-ins can re-weight them later. This is a Bayesian-update idea and not validated.
- **Readiness.** PC / C / P / A / M from item 8 (+8b). The "cut down first" answer maps to PC/C tone with a reduction plan offered.
- **EPPM.**
  - T = SEV + SUS (2–14), E = RE + SE (2–14), CV = E − T.
  - High = sum ≥10, meaning a mean of ≥5, "somewhat agree" or above. This threshold is not validated.
  - Quadrants:
    - High T / High E (danger control): standard risk + action messaging.
    - High T / Low E, or CV ≤ −3, or DA ≥5 (fear-control risk): efficacy-first, no graphic or loss-framed imagery, small wins.
    - Low T / High E: raise personal relevance with personalised risk and gain-framed benefits, using the "why it matters to you" approach.
    - Low T / Low E: low-pressure motivational content; build efficacy before introducing threat.

**C. Phenotype → dashboard layout and notification tone (rationale cited where evidence exists; the rest are theory-based proposals)**

| Classification | Dashboard emphasis (hero card first) | Notification tone and timing |
|---|---|---|
| High dependence (HSI 5–6 or TTFC ≤5 min) | Medication planner first: combination NRT (patch + fast-acting) and pre-quit NRT option, citing combination NRT RR 1.25 (Lindson 2019); withdrawal tracker | Practical and reassuring ("withdrawal peaks then fades"); morning-weighted, since TTFC shows morning is the high-risk window |
| AFFECT | Stress toolkit: breathing, urge-surfing / ACT-style defusion, mood check-in; "stress → urge" insight chart | Warm and validating; triggered by mood check-ins or user-defined stressful times, because negative affect rises hours before stress-attributed lapses |
| CUE | Trigger map: user-tagged places and routines, habit-swap plans; optional geofence alerts | Brief, specific, anticipatory ("coffee time — your swap is ready"); time- and place-anchored |
| AUTOMATIC | Self-monitoring log with friction tools (pack wrap, delay timer), streak counter; "cigarettes you didn't notice" awareness stats | Light, curious and playful; randomised "mindful moment" prompts to interrupt autopilot |
| SOCIAL | Social plan: event planner, refusal-script practice, buddy/support invites | Upbeat, peer-oriented; scheduled before known social events (Friday evenings, weekends) |
| Precontemplation / "cut down" | Motivation hub: personal reasons, money/health benefits, reduction goals; quit-plan CTA visible but not default (Cochrane: do not restrict advice by stage) | Non-judgmental, curiosity-based, low frequency; no quit-date pushes |
| Preparation | Quit-date setup, NRT ordering, pre-quit practice | Action-oriented countdown |
| EPPM fear-control risk | Lead with "how quitting works" success stats (response efficacy) and small wins; hide graphic risk cards | Encouraging, efficacy-first; suppress threat copy until E ≥10 on re-measurement |

- Tailoring effects are small. The design should therefore pair phenotype tailoring with ongoing interactive contact (missions and notifications, as in Smoke Free), not rely on onboarding classification alone. The Cochrane RR 1.42 for tailored multiple contacts, against RR 1.07 when contact is matched, suggests that most of the benefit comes from repeated contact.
- The phenotype-specific technique choices for CUE (stimulus control, geofences), AUTOMATIC (self-monitoring, friction) and SOCIAL (refusal skills) are theory-consistent but were **not** supported by phenotype-matching trial evidence retrieved here. Treat them as hypotheses for A/B tests, e.g. phenotype-matched vs generic hero card, with 7-day engagement and 30-day self-reported abstinence as outcomes.

### Gaps
- No trial evidence was found that matching interventions to smoking-motive phenotypes (affect/cue/automatic/social) improves cessation over unmatched delivery. This is the biggest evidence gap for the blueprint.
- Lustria 2013's pooled effect size was not retrieved. It is commonly cited as d ≈ 0.14, but that figure is unverified.
- Whittaker 2019's pooled RRs for text messaging and apps were not retrieved. The brief's "RR 1.54" was not verified.
- The Smoke Free pragmatic RCT results and the Crane missions effect estimates were not retrieved.
- Evidence on ACT apps for affect-driven smokers (e.g., iCanQuit vs QuitGuide, Bricker et al. 2020, *Lancet Digital Health*) and on geofencing/JITAI for cue-driven smokers was not retrieved before the search budget ran out.
- The proposed thresholds (EPPM sum ≥10, trigger "often" ≥3, tie-break order) are design choices. They need pilot calibration (e.g., distribution checks, test-retest, and agreement with the Brief WISDM and RBD in a validation sub-sample, with permission).

## Q8. Onboarding completion-time norms (seconds per item on mobile)

### Takeaway
I found no authoritative per-item seconds norm for single-choice mobile items. The cited survey-methods evidence gives only relative inflation: smartphones take about 1.1–1.4× as long as PCs, and more for multi-question pages, grids and open text. The commonly assumed 3–5 s per tap is unverified. The app should instrument item-level paradata and treat 90 s as a target to validate.

### Cited Findings
- 836 respondents who took the same survey on both smartphone and PC took about 1.4× longer on smartphones. The gap was larger for pages with more than one question or with text entry. — [RECSM Working Paper 46 (UPF)](https://www.upf.edu/documents/3966940/3986764/RECSM_wp046.pdf)
- SurveyMonkey: >3,000 U.S. respondents; mobile completers took 11% longer (6.28 vs 7.01 min) on a 21-question, mostly simple survey. — [SurveyMonkey "Why do surveys take longer on mobile"](https://surveymonkey.com/curiosity/why-do-surveys-take-longer-to-complete-on-mobile/)
- Grids vs item-by-item: one experiment found grids took longer for smartphone respondents. An ESRA 2017 study found grids were faster on both devices but with more measurement error. — [UPF repository](https://repositori.upf.edu/items/63708937-1d64-4ea1-a98f-78faa1a3017b/full); [Mavletova et al. ESRA 2017](https://hse.ru/data/2017/10/15/1159283029/Mavletova%20et%20al_Grids_item-by-item_ESRA_2017.pdf)
- Open-ended items add the most time on phones because of small keyboards. — [Ipsos "4 Myths about mobile respondents"](https://www.ipsos.com/sites/default/files/2017-03/Ipsos_IIS_4MythsMobileRespondents.pdf)

### Inferences
- From the SurveyMonkey figures: 7.01 min ≈ 421 s over 21 questions ≈ 20 s per question on mobile. Those were general survey questions with longer stems and possibly multi-part formats, so this is an upper-bound reference, not a norm for one-tap onboarding items.
- To meet <90 s with 12–15 taps, each screen should have a short stem (≤15 words), ≤6 large tap targets, auto-advance on selection, no grids and no free text. The 4 trigger items should use identical anchors to minimise re-reading, and the 4 EPPM items likewise.
- Recommended telemetry: per-screen dwell time, back-navigation rate, straight-lining across the trigger and EPPM blocks (e.g., all identical answers, which is a data-quality flag), and abandonment by item.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed absolute timing norm (seconds per single-choice item on smartphones) was retrieved. Couper & Peterson (2017, *Soc Sci Comput Rev*) on why web surveys take longer on smartphones was not retrieved before the search budget ran out.
- No completion-time data for existing smoking-cessation app onboarding flows (e.g., Smoke Free, QuitGuide) was found.
