## Q1. Carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) and exhaled CO: levels by cigarettes/day, per-cigarette increment, CFK equation, elimination half-life, ppm↔COHb conversion, SRNT cutoffs, and a usable superposition model

### Takeaway
A first-order superposition model for COHb/exhaled CO is the single best-evidenced component of the index: the decay is empirically mono-exponential with a **daytime half-life of ~4.5 h** (Sandberg 2011, r² = 0.7) and a **substantially longer half-life during sleep** (~8 h in men, n = 5 — very weak), with ventilation as the mechanistic driver. However, the *absolute calibration* layer — COHb% by cigarettes/day, the per-cigarette ΔCOHb increment, and an exact published ppm→COHb regression for smokers — could not be retrieved in this pass and is the single biggest numeric gap in Section 1.2.

### Cited Findings

**Elimination kinetics (strongest evidence)**
- Exhaled CO decline after the last cigarette is well fit by a **mono-exponential decay (r² = 0.7) with a half-life of 4.5 hours**; a separate slope estimate in the same paper gives **4.3 hours**, both computed as ln(2)/slope — Sandberg et al. 2011, "Assessing Recent Smoking Status by Measuring Exhaled Carbon Monoxide Levels", *PLoS ONE*, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0028864 — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0028864); [PubMed 22194931](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/22194931/)
- The same paper warns the decay rate is **not** a population constant: "CO decline rate depends on initial CO levels, and by necessity a generic cut-off is therefore crude as initial CO_breath varies a lot between individuals" — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0028864)
- A **12 ppm** cut-off separated recent smokers from smokers abstinent ~8 h with **specificity 94%, sensitivity 90%** — Sandberg et al. 2011 — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0028864)
- **Sleep roughly doubles the half-life, and there is a sex difference**: in healthy volunteers, 2-point post-smoking t½ was **8.0 ± 1.6 h in men (n = 5)** vs **4.3 ± 1.1 h in women (n = 4)**, attributed to reduced alveolar ventilation during sleep — ["The elimination of carboxyhemoglobin — gender-specific and circadian effects", PubMed 1498552](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1498552/). **Sample sizes of 4 and 5. Treat as hypothesis-generating only.**
- Moderately heavy smokers "rise in the morning with a substantially raised COHb level because the half life of COHb is significantly longer during sleep than during the day" — ["Variations in Carboxyhaemoglobin Levels in Smokers", PMC1612791](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1612791/)
- **Exercise accelerates clearance**: "performing even mild exercise (coupled with the concomitant hyperventilation) seems to sharply reduce the half-life of COHb", citing Zavorsky et al. 2012 — review ["Tobacco smoking: A stimulus for blood volume expansion"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1569904825001168). *I did not reach the Zavorsky primary source; no quantitative factor retrieved.*
- **100% O₂ at 1 atm**: Weaver LK, Howe S, Hopkins R, Chan KJ. "Carboxyhemoglobin half-life in carbon monoxide-poisoned patients treated with 100% oxygen at atmospheric pressure." *Chest* 2000;117(3):801–808 — citation confirmed via [US EPA HERO record 16421](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/16421/). A citing review reports the value as **COHb t½ = 74 ± 25 min (mean ± SD)** and notes the study was **retrospective with missing early COHb measurements**, which may bias the early elimination phase — [Carboxyhemoglobin half-life toxicokinetic profiles during and after normobaric oxygen therapy (swine model)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S2214750024000192). **Conflict:** a separate case report characterises the 74-min figure as deriving from **adult volunteers** rather than patients — [ResearchGate case report](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/321795388_Carboxyhemoglobin_half-life_during_hyperbaric_oxygen_in_a_patient_with_lung_dysfunction_A_case_report). Not resolvable without the full text.
- **Room air ~320 min (5.3 h)** is the widely circulated clinical figure, with ~80 min on a non-rebreather at 100% FiO₂ and ~23 min at 3 ATA hyperbaric — [FPNotebook](https://fpnotebook.com/ER/Lab/Crbxyhmglbn.htm); same 320 min room-air value in a hospital lab manual — [SBGH lab manual](https://apps.sbgh.mb.ca/labmanual/test/view?seedId=54); and an OSHA interpretation states "One hundred percent O₂ at 1 atmosphere reduces the half-life from about 320 minutes to about 80" — [OSHA standard interpretation 1991-04-08-2](https://www.osha.gov/laws-regs/standardinterpretations/1991-04-08-2). **These are tertiary/clinical-reference sources, not primary data.** WikEM gives a rougher ~5 h room air / ~90 min on 100% O₂ / ~30 min HBO — [WikEM Carboxyhemoglobin](https://www.wikem.org/wiki/Carboxyhemoglobin). A general reference puts the typical human half-life at ~300 min and cites a reported human range of **22–360 min** — [Wikipedia: Carboxyhemoglobin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carboxyhemoglobin) (tertiary; listed only because it is the source of the 22–360 min range I saw).
- **Mono-exponential is a simplification**: a sheep study found "short-term exposures exhibited biphasic decreases of COHb concentration" with a rapid initial phase — Shimazu et al. 2000, *J Trauma*, "Half-life of blood carboxyhemoglobin after short-term and long-term exposure to carbon monoxide" — [reference record](https://m.jci.org/references/scholar/22208/B40). An ovine smoke-inhalation model likewise reports an elimination curve with arterial–venous differences in the acute phase — [Biochem Biophys Res Commun](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0006291X20305283)

**Smoker vs non-smoker levels and CPD dependence**
- Wald NJ, Idle M, Boreham J, Bailey A. "Carbon monoxide in breath in relation to smoking and carboxyhaemoglobin levels." *Thorax* 1981;36(5):366–369, DOI 10.1136/thx.36.5.366. COHb was measured in **11,249 men**; the distribution in the **2,613 cigarette smokers** was "well separated" from that in **6,641 non-smokers (including ex-smokers)**. At a **COHb cut-off of 2%**: **81% of cigarette smokers, 35% of cigar/pipe smokers and 1.0% of non-smokers** were above it. A subsidiary experiment collected alveolar air from **162 smokers and 25 non-smokers** — [Oxford ORA record](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid:e5e6ee8f-90e7-4c98-b651-1d63086ad7e9); [PMC471511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC471511)
- A 2025 retrospective observational study specifically titled "**Effect of collection time and reported cigarettes per day on exhaled carbon monoxide**" exists and is the most directly relevant modern source for the CPD→CO dose term — [PMC13536744](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13536744/). **I could not retrieve its numbers.** This is the highest-value single fetch for a follow-up pass.

**ppm ↔ COHb conversion**
- Andersson JE, Møller AM 2010, "Assessment of carbon monoxide values in smokers: a comparison of carbon monoxide in expired air and carboxyhaemoglobin in arterial blood", *Eur J Anaesthesiol*, DOI 10.1097/EJA.0b013e32833a55ea. **n = 37 smokers.** Micro Smokerlyzer CO_exp vs arterial COHb on an ABL 725: "The correlation between the methods was high (rho = 0.964). Bland-Altman analysis demonstrated that the Micro Smokerlyzer **underestimated** COHb values. The two methods showed a high level of correlation, but **poor agreement**… we suggested an **alternative algorithm** for converting CO_exp from ppm to %Hb." ROC AUC for separating heavy from light smokers: **0.746 (ABL 725) vs 0.754 (Micro Smokerlyzer), P = 0.815** — [Ovid abstract](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/ejanaesthesiology/abstract/10.1097/eja.0b013e32833a55ea~assessment-of-carbon-monoxide-values-in-smokers-a-comparison?redirectionsource=fulltextview); [PubMed 20520557](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pubmed/20520557). **The alternative algorithm itself is behind the full text and was not retrieved.**
- Later work cites this as the clinical validation of the Smokerlyzer against COHb: "It was validated in the clinical setting against carboxyhemoglobin (COHb) concentrations (Andersson and Møller, 2010) and other methods (Moscato et al., 2014) with good overall correlation" — [Sandberg et al. 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0028864)
- **A regression does exist but is from the wrong population:** in 47 **acute CO-poisoning patients**, `%HbCO = 10.315 + 0.203 × (ppm CO)`, r = 0.835 — [Seoul National University thesis record](https://s-space.snu.ac.kr/handle/10371/9604). **Do not use for smokers**: the intercept of 10.3% is nonsensical for a smoker baseline and the slope (0.203 %/ppm) is ~25% steeper than the commonly quoted ~0.16 %/ppm rule of thumb. **The ~0.16 %/ppm figure in the brief could not be traced to a primary source in this pass.**

**SRNT / verification cutoffs**
- Benowitz NL et al. 2020, "Biochemical Verification of Tobacco Use and Abstinence: 2019 Update", *Nicotine & Tobacco Research* 22(7):1086–1097, DOI 10.1093/ntr/ntz132. Author-manuscript copy hosted by CDC — [stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/103507](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/103507/cdc_103507_DS1.pdf). Stated position: "Biochemical verification of tobacco use and abstinence increases scientific rigor and is recommended in clinical trials of smoking cessation, when feasible."
- A later meta-analysis characterises the 2020 update as **lowering the recommended CO threshold for smoking-cessation trials to 5–6 ppm**, and as recommending that **all studies report which CO monitor model was used**; it also notes the recommendation rests on a **narrative literature synthesis** rather than direct measurement of how cutoffs affect quit rates — [J Environ Expo Assess 2025](https://www.oaepublish.com/articles/jeea.2025.69). **This is a secondary characterisation of the 5–6 ppm number; verify against the NTR full text before shipping it as the app's threshold.**
- Historical breath-CO abstinence cutoffs ranged from **3–4 ppm up to 7–10 ppm**, and the appropriate cutoff "may depend largely on the type of CO monitor being used" — context from the cutoff literature; see the UCL manuscript on expired-air CO thresholds — [UCL Discovery 1467536](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1467536/1/FINAL%20MANUSCRIPT_ADMENDED%20WITH%20AGE_ROBERT_190315clean.pdf), and an empirical cutoff-optimisation study in Korean Americans — [PMC8279201](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8279201/)
- Expired-air CO is preferred operationally because it is "less costly, less invasive to obtain, and not affected by the use of nicotine replacement products", while cotinine remains "the best biomarker for validating smoking cessation except for individuals using nicotine-containing medications or other tobacco products"; CO's short half-life means it "is typically used to evaluate past 24-hour tobacco use" — [PMC8026538](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8026538); [PMC7976935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7976935)
- A standardised measurement protocol for expired CO suitable for app/clinic use is published by PhenX — [PhenX protocol 720301](https://www.phenxtoolkit.org/protocols/view/720301)

### Inferences
- **The superposition model the brief proposes is sound and directly supported for exhaled CO, with one required modification: a circadian/activity-dependent τ.** Form:

  `CO_breath(t) = C_env + Σ_i ΔCO_i · exp( −(t − t_i)/τ(t) )`
  `COHb(t)      = COHb_env + Σ_i ΔCOHb_i · exp( −(t − t_i)/τ(t) )`

  with `τ = t½ / ln2`. Using only the values I could source:
  - **Awake/daytime:** t½ = 4.5 h → **τ_awake = 6.49 h** (Sandberg 2011, r² = 0.7 — the only primary half-life I verified in a smoking, non-poisoned population).
  - **Asleep:** t½ = 8.0 h → **τ_sleep = 11.54 h** (men, n = 5) and t½ = 4.3 h → **τ_sleep = 6.20 h** (women, n = 4). **Flag as weak (n < 6 per cell).** A defensible engineering compromise is a single sleep multiplier of **×1.5–1.8 on τ**, declared in-app as an approximation.
  - **Exercise:** direction is established (↑ ventilation → ↓ t½) but **no magnitude was verified**; do not implement a quantitative exercise accelerator yet.
  - **100% O₂ branch** (t½ 74 min) is clinically irrelevant for this app and should not appear.
- Because the half-life is 4.3–4.5 h awake, **CO is a near-perfect "today" signal and a useless "this month" signal**: after 24 h of abstinence (~5.3 awake half-lives) the smoking-attributable CO burden is down to ~2.5% of peak. That is exactly the right time constant for a daily-streak mechanic and exactly the wrong one for a long-term "damage" narrative. The 8–12 h "CO normalises" folk claim in quit timelines is consistent with ~2 half-lives, i.e. ~75% clearance, not full normalisation.
- **ΔCOHb per cigarette must be calibrated from the CPD→steady-state relationship, not from a published per-cigarette increment** (which I could not find). If the engineering team obtains a steady-state COHb (or CO ppm) vs CPD curve, the per-cigarette increment is recoverable analytically: for `n` cigarettes/day spread over a `T_wake`-hour waking window, steady-state peak ≈ `Δ / (1 − exp(−Δt/τ))` with `Δt = T_wake/n`; inverting gives Δ. **Implement the dose term as a fitted parameter with a published steady-state anchor, not as an invented per-cigarette constant.**
- Because Sandberg explicitly warns the decline rate covaries with initial CO, a **per-user τ fitted from the user's own Smokerlyzer readings** (if the app pairs with a breath CO device) would be more accurate than the population constant — and is a genuine product differentiator.
- The Andersson & Møller result has a direct product consequence: **consumer Smokerlyzer-class devices systematically under-read COHb**, so the app should present **ppm (the measured quantity)** and avoid displaying a converted COHb% unless it adopts a named, cited conversion.

### Gaps
- **The Coburn–Forster–Kane equation was not retrieved in algebraic form.** Coburn RF, Forster RE, Kane PB 1965, *J Clin Invest* 44(11):1899–1910, DOI 10.1172/JCI105250 — the citation in the brief is plausible but I could not open the paper, and I will not reconstruct a differential equation with its Haldane constant, V_b, D_L, P_L, V̇_CO and endogenous-production terms from memory. **This must be fetched before any CFK-based implementation.** Note also that CFK is a mass-balance model for *continuous* exposure; for discrete cigarette boluses the empirical first-order superposition above is the more appropriate engineering choice regardless.
- **COHb% in smokers stratified by cigarettes/day** (the "~3–8% vs <1–2%" claim): not found. Wald 1981 gives only the fraction above a 2% cut-off, not means by CPD. The commonly cited Jarvis/Wald stratified table was not reached.
- **Jarvis MJ et al. 1986 (Thorax) ppm→COHb regression coefficients**: not retrieved. The brief's "COHb% ≈ 0.16 × ppm" could not be traced to any primary source in this pass. **Flag in the app as unsourced until verified.**
- **Per-cigarette ΔCOHb (%) or ΔCO (ppm) increment**: no primary value found.
- **Quantitative effect of exercise on COHb t½** (the Zavorsky 2012 figure): not retrieved.
- **Weaver 2000 full text**: needed to resolve whether 74 ± 25 min came from patients or volunteers, and whether the paper reports any room-air comparison group at all.
- **Endogenous CO production rate** (the baseline/offset term `COHb_env`, from haem catabolism, typically needed to set the non-smoker floor): no sourced value.
- **Ambient-CO confounding**: one paper indicates air pollution can affect exhaled-CO-based smoking assessment — [Environ Res](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0013935119300349) — but no correction magnitude was retrieved. Relevant for urban users.

## Q2. Nicotine and cotinine half-lives, and how they map to the withdrawal timeline

### Takeaway
Nicotine's ~2 h half-life is confirmed only via a secondary source citing the Benowitz/Hukkanen/Jacob chapter; **the 16 h cotinine half-life asserted in the brief was NOT verified in this pass** — the only metabolite half-life I could source from these results is ~6 h for trans-3′-hydroxycotinine, which is a different compound. This is a mandatory re-check before the withdrawal-timeline mapping is built.

### Cited Findings
- Benowitz NL, Hukkanen J, Jacob P 3rd. "Nicotine Chemistry, Metabolism, Kinetics and Biomarkers." *Handb Exp Pharmacol* 2009;192:29–60, DOI 10.1007/978-3-540-69248-5_2 (in *Nicotine Psychopharmacology*) — citation confirmed — [Springer chapter PDF landing](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-540-69248-5_2.pdf); [ARUP reference 19184645](https://arupconsult.com/reference/19184645)
- **Nicotine half-life ≈ 2 h**: "the half-life of nicotine on average is two hours" — secondary source citing the above chapter — [ARUP Consult](https://arupconsult.com/reference/19184645). **Provenance: secondary. The chapter full text was not reachable.**
- Metabolic route: nicotine is metabolised "primarily by the liver enzymes CYP2A6, UDP-glucuronosyltransferase (UGT), and flavin-containing monooxygenase (FMO)", with "oxidation of the 5′-carbon atom to give the γ-lactam derivative cotinine" as the main pathway in most mammals — chapter abstract via [Springer](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-3-540-69248-5_2.pdf)
- **trans-3′-hydroxycotinine plasma half-life ≈ 6 h** — appeared in the retrieved abstract material for the nicotine-metabolism literature; see [J Biol Chem review, "Biochemistry of nicotine metabolism and its relevance to lung cancer"](https://www.jbc.org/article/S0021-9258(21)00511-1/fulltext). **Note: this is 3HC, not cotinine.**
- Adolescent-specific nicotine metabolism and dependence is a distinct literature, directly relevant to a 20–25-year-old target user — [PMC3504133, "Nicotine metabolism and addiction among adolescent smokers"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3504133/). **Content not retrieved.**
- Operational consequence already noted in the verification literature: because cotinine's half-life is long relative to CO's, **cotinine is the stronger abstinence biomarker except in people using nicotine-containing medications**, while CO "is typically used to evaluate past 24-hour tobacco use" — [PMC8026538](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8026538); [PMC7976935](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7976935)

### Inferences
- With t½(nicotine) ≈ 2 h → **τ_nic ≈ 2.89 h**. This is the right time constant for the *urge/craving* channel, and it is ~2.2× faster than the CO channel. That divergence is a product opportunity, not a nuisance: **the app can honestly show "the thing driving your urge right now is 2.9 h-τ nicotine; the thing poisoning your oxygen transport is 6.5 h-τ CO"**, which explains to the user why cravings spike before the toxicity number has meaningfully moved.
- Nicotine's ~2 h half-life means **~8–10 h of sleep spans ~4–5 half-lives**, so a smoker wakes nicotine-depleted (~3–6% of bedtime level) but **not** CO-depleted (~1 awake-equivalent half-life plus slowed sleep clearance). This asymmetry is a clean, mechanistic explanation for the morning cigarette and should be used in onboarding copy.
- **Do not build the withdrawal-timeline mapping on the 16 h cotinine figure until it is verified.** If it holds, τ_cot ≈ 23 h and cotinine is a ~3–5 day clearance signal (5 half-lives ≈ 80 h), which maps onto the acute withdrawal window — but that chain of reasoning is currently resting on an unverified number.

### Gaps
- **Cotinine elimination half-life (the brief's ~16 h) — NOT VERIFIED.** No retrieved source stated it. Needs the Benowitz 2009 chapter or Benowitz 2009 *Clin Pharmacol Ther*.
- Nicotine **distribution-phase** half-life, clearance (L/min), volume of distribution, and arterial vs venous peak concentrations per cigarette: not retrieved.
- Typical plasma nicotine and cotinine concentrations in smokers by CPD, and the per-cigarette plasma nicotine increment: not retrieved.
- Time to nicotine steady state and intraday accumulation profile: not retrieved.
- **Quantitative mapping of nicotine/cotinine kinetics onto a withdrawal-symptom timeline** (peak craving hour, irritability trajectory): I found no source linking the PK parameters to a symptom curve. This is a genuine evidence gap, not just a retrieval failure — flag as extrapolated in the index.
- CYP2A6-driven inter-individual variability (fast vs slow metabolisers) and its effect on τ: not retrieved, though it is known to be large and would widen any per-user model's error bars.

## Q3. Mucociliary clearance, ciliary structure and function, and their recovery after cessation

### Takeaway
This is the **best domain in the whole index for a young low-pack-year smoker**, because the deficit is measurable, structural, and — uniquely — has a *published, dated recovery inflection*: nasal saccharin transit time returns to the normal range by **day 15** after quitting and stays normal through 180 days (Ramos 2011, n = 33, single-arm). Cilia are **7–15% shorter** in healthy smokers (Leopold 2009). The widely repeated "cilia recover in 1–9 months" claim, however, traces only to secondary sources paraphrasing the 1990 Surgeon General report — the cilia wording does not appear in the WHO's rendering of that timeline.

### Cited Findings

**Structure: cilia length and ciliary gene expression**
- Leopold PL, O'Mahony MJ, Lian XJ, Tilley AE, Harvey B-G, Crystal RG. "Smoking Is Associated with Shortened Airway Cilia." *PLoS ONE* 2009;4(12):e8157, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0008157 (published 16 Dec 2009; Weill Cornell Medical College). Cilia were **~7% to 15% shorter in healthy smokers than nonsmokers** across sample types, with the **largest reduction (15%) in endobronchial biopsies (n = 28 biopsies)**. **6 cilia-related genes** were significantly reduced in healthy smokers vs healthy nonsmokers. The hypothesis was motivated by models predicting cilia must extend beyond the airway surface fluid to move mucus — [PMC2790614](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2790614/); [DOAJ record](https://doaj.org/article/9ae3ebb167754a9f99c554001f70d5d9)
- **Important methodological caveat:** the reduced-clearance conclusion "rests on models predicting reduced mucociliary clearance, not direct measurement of clearance." The paper's Figure 5 is explicitly titled "**Theoretical** effect of smoking on ciliary contribution to mucociliary clearance" — [PLOS figshare, Figure 5](https://plos.figshare.com/articles/figure/_Theoretical_effect_of_smoking_on_ciliary_contribution_to_mucociliary_clearance_/542104)
- Follow-up: cilia are shorter in the large airway in healthy smokers than nonsmokers, and shorter still in smokers with COPD, with intraflagellar transport gene expression implicated — Hessel et al. 2014, *PLoS ONE* — [PMC3896362](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3896362/)
- Verra F, Escudier E, Lebargy F, Bernaudin JF, De Cremoux H, Bignon J. "Ciliary Abnormalities in Bronchial Epithelium of Smokers, Ex-Smokers, and Nonsmokers." *Am J Respir Crit Care Med* 1995;151:630–634, DOI 10.1164/ajrccm/151.3_Pt_1.630 — **citation confirmed, content NOT retrieved** — [SciRP reference record](https://scirp.org/reference/referencespapers.aspx?referenceid=1789674). Later papers cite it among studies finding cilia shortened in healthy smokers vs non-smokers, "likely to contribute significantly to impaired mucociliary clearance."
- Cigarette smoke exposure impairs respiratory epithelial **ciliogenesis** (i.e. the regeneration process itself, not just mature cilia) — Tamashiro E, Xiong G, Anselmo-Lima WT, Kreindler JL, Palmer JN, Cohen NA 2009, *Am J Rhinol Allergy*, DOI 10.2500/ajra.2009.23.3280 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.2500/ajra.2009.23.3280)
- Repeated air–liquid-interface exposure of human primary bronchiolar epithelial cells to mainstream smoke produces measurable ciliatoxicity — [Exp Toxicol Pathol](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0940299315000706)

**Function: saccharin transit time (STT) and recovery after cessation — THE KEY RECOVERY CURVE**
- **Ramos EMC et al. (Cipulo Ramos) 2011, "Reversibility of impaired nasal mucociliary clearance in smokers following a smoking cessation programme", *Respirology* 16(5):849–855, DOI 10.1111/j.1440-1843.2011.01985.x, PMID 21545372.** 33 current smokers in a cessation programme vs 33 matched non-smokers. "STT was prolonged in smokers compared with non-smokers (**P = 0.002**)"; "**STT values decreased to within the normal range on day 15 after SC (P < 0.01), and remained in the normal range until the end of the study period**"; conclusion: "A SC programme contributed to improvement in MC among smokers **from the 15th day** after cessation of smoking, and these beneficial effects **persisted for 180 days**." Abstinence attrition: **75.9% (n = 23) at 15 days, 62.7% (n = 19) at 30 days, 49.5% (n = 15) at 60/90 days, 30% (n = 11) at 120 days, 27% (n = 9) at 180 days**. Baseline MC dysfunction was present in both eventual-abstainers and non-abstainers — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1440-1843.2011.01985.x); [PubMed 21545372](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21545372)
- **Cross-sectional ex-smoker vs current-smoker STT (gives the plateau magnitude):** Ito JT et al. 2015, "Nasal Mucociliary Clearance in Subjects With COPD After Smoking Cessation", *Respir Care*, DOI 10.4187/respcare.03266, n = 26 COPD. Median [IQR 25–75%] STT: **smokers with COPD 16.5 [11–28] min; current smokers 15.9 [10–27] min; ex-smokers with COPD 9.7 [6–12] min; nonsmokers 8 [6–16] min (P < .001)** — [SAGE/Respir Care](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.4187/respcare.03266)
- **Test–retest reliability (needed if the app ever asks users to self-test):** 39 current, 40 former and 40 never smokers; "significant repeatability in both current and former smokers" over 3 and 30 days. The same authors state plainly that "it is not known if MCC impairment can be reversed when stopping smoking" — [PMC7537769, "Short and Long Term Repeatability of Saccharin Transit Time in Current, Former, and Never Smokers"](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7537769/). **This directly tempers the Ramos result.**
- Recent (2021) corroboration in people who had quit 3–36 months prior: "MCC restoration after smoking cessation appears to occur within a relatively short time period after quitting", with the explicit caveat that "prospective studies are required to clarify the time-course of MCCTT restoration after smoking cessation" — Polosa R, Emma R, Cibella F, Caruso M, Conte G, Benfatto F, Ferlito S, Gulino A, Malerba M, Caponnetto P 2021, *Ther Adv Chronic Dis*, DOI 10.1177/20406223211035267 — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.1177/20406223211035267). **Note industry-funding sensitivities in this author group's e-cigarette work; the cessation-recovery observation is nonetheless consistent with Ramos.**
- Systematic review of nasal MCC in smokers — [PMC7851360](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7851360/); [SciELO version](https://scielo.br/j/iao/a/y9xL8KWxpNsPWzxcyXJdZBg/?lang=en)
- Cytologic and functional alterations of nasal mucosa in smokers — Pagliuca G et al. 2015, *Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg*, DOI 10.1177/0194599814566598 — [SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0194599814566598)
- Bronchial epithelial remodelling in relation to smoking cessation in COPD — [PMC2214729](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2214729/) (content not retrieved; lead for epithelial regeneration timing)

**The "1–9 months" claim and its provenance**
- WHO's health-benefits-of-cessation timeline, which cites the 1990 US Surgeon General report *The Health Benefits of Smoking Cessation*, states: "**1–9 months, coughing and shortness of breath decrease**" — [WHO Q&A: Tobacco — Health benefits of smoking cessation](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation)
- The American Lung Association uses the same window ("**1 to 9 Months After Quitting**") — [ALA, Benefits of Quitting](https://www.lung.org/quit-smoking/i-want-to-quit/benefits-of-quitting). The American Cancer Society uses a **wider** window: "**1 to 12 months after quitting. Coughing and shortness of breath decrease**" — [ACS, Health Benefits of Quitting Smoking Over Time](https://www.cancer.org/cancer/risk-prevention/tobacco/guide-quitting-smoking/benefits-of-quitting-smoking-over-time.html). **The two major US bodies disagree on the upper bound (9 vs 12 months).**
- **The cilia-specific wording is secondary-source only.** Consumer health sites render it as "1 to 9 months after quitting: … cilia … start to regain normal function in the lungs", attributing it to the 1990 report's pages, and a Canadian public-health handout "adapted from" the 1990 report is more hedged: "potential for cilia to regrow in lungs, increasing ability to handle mucus, clean the lungs and reduce infection" — [WDMH patient handout](https://www.wdmh.on.ca/uploads/Common/1791_Health%20Benefits%20of%20Quitting%20Smoking_Accessible.pdf). **WHO's rendering of the same 1990 timeline does not mention cilia at all** — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation). Other secondary sources give yet other windows ("largely restored by 3–9 months"), which is a sign of uncontrolled paraphrase.

### Inferences
- **The MCC recovery curve is a step, not an exponential, in the published data.** Ramos reports STT inside the normal range *by day 15* with no reported intermediate trajectory. For the index, the honest implementations are either (a) a **saturating exponential with τ ≈ 5–7 days**, chosen so that ~85–95% of recovery has occurred by day 15 (this τ is **our engineering choice, not published**), or (b) a literal **two-state step at day 15**. I recommend (a) for UX smoothness with an explicit in-app note that only the day-15 endpoint is evidenced.
- **Magnitude anchor for the display:** combining Ito's cross-sectional medians, current smoking is associated with roughly **a doubling of nasal transit time (≈ 16 min vs ≈ 8–10 min)**. That is a far more motivating number for a 22-year-old than a null FEV1 — and it is a *function*, not a risk projection.
- **Confidence should be marked medium, not high.** Ramos is n = 33, single-arm, no randomisation, with 73% attrition by 180 days, so the day-180 subgroup (n = 9) is self-selected; and the 2020 repeatability authors state outright that reversibility "is not known". Leopold's clearance claim is explicitly theoretical. The cilia-length data are cross-sectional only — **no study I retrieved measures cilia length before and after quitting in the same people**, so "your cilia are regrowing" is an *extrapolation* from (i) cross-sectional length deficits, (ii) functional STT recovery, and (iii) in-vitro ciliogenesis impairment. Copy should say "clearance function recovers" (evidenced) rather than "your cilia regrow by day X" (not evidenced).
- **The "1–9 months" line should be presented as a guideline-level consensus statement, not a measurement.** Its upper bound is disputed (9 vs 12 months) and its cilia gloss is a secondary-source accretion. If the app cites it, cite WHO or ACS by name rather than implying a primary study.
- **Transient post-quit cough worsening is a real retention risk and I could not source it.** The brief's Ussher 2003 reference (increased common-cold symptoms and mouth ulcers after cessation, *Tob Control*) was not retrievable before the search budget ran out. This matters operationally: if the app's MCC curve goes *up* (better) while the user's cough goes *up* (worse) in the first weeks, unexplained, the user will distrust the index. **Fetch Ussher 2003 before shipping the MCC module** and add an explicit "cough may transiently increase — this is clearance restarting" explainer.

### Gaps
- **Ussher M, West R, Steptoe A, McEwen A 2003, "Increase in common cold symptoms and mouth ulcers following smoking cessation", *Tob Control*** — not retrieved (search budget exhausted). Incidence figures and time window unknown.
- **Exact STT values in minutes at days 15/30/60/90/120/180 in Ramos 2011** — abstract states "within the normal range" but the per-timepoint table is behind the full text.
- **Absolute cilia lengths in µm** (nonsmoker vs smoker, by sample type) in Leopold 2009 — only the percentage reductions were retrieved.
- **Verra 1995 content** — the only retrieved source that explicitly includes an *ex-smoker* arm in a ciliary-structure study, and I could not open it. High-value fetch.
- **Airway epithelial regeneration time (days–weeks)** — no sourced value found. The brief's "~days–weeks" is unsupported in my results.
- **Primary tracing of the 1990 Surgeon General cilia statement** to CDC publication 90-8416 page level — not done; cdc.gov was unreachable.
- **Lung Health Study symptom-resolution trajectories** and Wynder's cough data — not searched (budget).
- **Tracheobronchial (not nasal) MCC recovery after cessation** — e.g. radioaerosol clearance studies; not retrieved. All recovery data above is *nasal* STT, and generalising nasal → bronchial is an assumption.

## Q4. Acute endothelial dysfunction and arterial stiffness: FMD, PWV, augmentation index — magnitude, duration, recovery

### Takeaway
The acute vascular hit from a single cigarette is well documented and **appears within 15 minutes** (AIx, PWV, BP — Mahmud & Feely 2003), with FMD falling by **0.88–1.17 percentage points** per cigarette (Amato 2013). Recovery after cessation is supported by a 2025 systematic review (improvements in PWV, AIx and FMD, "some effects observable within one month and sustained up to 24 months", moderate certainty for FMD from RCTs). **The duration of the acute single-cigarette FMD depression — the parameter the index most needs — was not found in any retrieved source.**

### Cited Findings

**Acute, within-minutes effects**
- Mahmud A, Feely J. "Effect of smoking on arterial stiffness and pulse pressure amplification." *Hypertension* 2003;41(1):183–187, DOI 10.1161/01.HYP.0000047464.66901.60. **28 subjects (11 chronic smokers)** measured "before and for **15 minutes** after smoking 1 cigarette (nicotine content, **1.2 mg**)". "Acutely in both groups, smoking significantly increased (**P < 0.01**) both brachial and aortic blood pressure, augmentation index, and pulse wave velocity. **No changes were seen after sham smoking.**" Methods: AIx by applanation tonometry (SphygmoCor), aortic PWV by Complior — [AHA Journals](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/01.hyp.0000047464.66901.60); [PubMed 12511550](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12511550/)
- Same paper, **chronic effect in young adults (41 chronic smokers vs 116 nonsmokers)**: brachial BP did not differ, but "the aortic systolic blood pressure (**101 ± 8 versus 97 ± 9 mm Hg**) and augmentation index (**0.7 ± 13 versus −5.7 ± 14**) were higher (**P < 0.01**) in chronic smokers than in nonsmokers, whereas aortic-brachial pulse pressure amplification was reduced (**13.7 ± 8 versus 17.7 ± 5 mm Hg, P < 0.01**)." Authors attribute the amplification loss "largely as a consequence of increased arterial stiffness" — [AHA Journals](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/01.hyp.0000047464.66901.60). **This is the single most useful finding in the whole brief for the "young smoker with normal spirometry" problem: a measurable chronic abnormality in young adults where brachial BP looks normal.**
- A 2010 systematic review confirms "acute smoking was found to cause an acute increase in arterial stiffness" — [Hypertens Res / Nature](https://www.nature.com/articles/hr201025)
- **Single-cigarette FMD drop: −0.88% (regular) and −1.17% (light), both P < 0.05**, with no significant difference between cigarette types — Amato et al. 2013, "Effects of smoking regular or light cigarettes on brachial artery flow-mediated dilation", *Atherosclerosis* — [PubMed 23528831](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23528831/); [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0021915013001731)
- Review-level: "decreased flow-mediated dilation (FMD) in subjects after smoking only one cigarette, but also in chronic smokers" — [PMC10264470, "Tobacco smoking and vascular biology and function: evidence from human studies"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10264470/)
- In young adults, after acute cigarette-smoke exposure "arterial dilation and brachial flow velocity were lower than those achieved in the abstinence phase" — [Can J Physiol Pharmacol 2019, DOI 10.1139/cjpp-2019-0187](https://cdnsciencepub.com/doi/abs/10.1139/cjpp-2019-0187)
- **Habitual smoking in young adults is vessel-bed-specific**: shear-mediated dilation was attenuated in the **brachial** artery but **not** the carotid artery — Suzuki et al. 2020, *Physiological Reports*, DOI 10.14814/phy2.14369 — [Wiley](https://physoc.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.14814/phy2.14369); [PMC7023886](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7023886/)
- Passive smoking also alters BP and the aortic pressure waveform in healthy young adults — [Br J Clin Pharmacol 2003, PMC1884425](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1884425/)
- Acute hookah smoking increases arterial stiffness and wave reflections in adults aged 18–34 — [Am J Cardiol](https://www.ajconline.org/article/S0002-9149(18)31206-2/abstract) (relevant if the app covers alternative products)

**Recovery after cessation**
- Johnson HM et al., "Effects of Smoking and Smoking Cessation on Endothelial Function: One-Year Outcomes From a Randomized Clinical Trial". Attribution supported by a University of Wisconsin seminar listing (4 Feb 2010) with Heather Johnson presenting that exact title — [today.wisc.edu event 26008](https://today.wisc.edu/events/view/26008). Matching study record: "a one-year, prospective, double-blind, randomized, placebo-controlled clinical trial of the effects of **5 smoking cessation pharmacotherapies**"; "FMD was measured by B-mode ultrasound **before and 1-year after** the target smoking cessation date"; **n = 1,504 current smokers (58% female, 84% white)**; "Cessation was verified by exhaled carbon monoxide levels" — [PMC2947952](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2947952). **The record retrieved did not show journal/authors, so the JACC 2010 citation in the brief is PLAUSIBLE BUT UNCONFIRMED, and the FMD change values were not retrieved.**
- **2025 systematic review (the key recent source):** "smoking cessation was consistently associated with improvements in **PWV, AIx, and FMD**, with some effects observable **within one month** and sustained **up to 24 months**", rating the evidence for FMD from RCTs as **moderate certainty** — "Vascular health after quitting smoking or switching to e-cigarettes" — [University of Dundee Discovery record](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/vascular-health-after-quitting-smoking-or-switching-to-e-cigarett/)
- A 2024 meta-analysis of smoking exposure and FMD exists and would supply pooled effect sizes — [PMC11151634](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11151634/). **Numbers not retrieved.**
- Chronic e-cigarette use and FMD, systematic review/meta-analysis — [PMC11110651](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11110651/)
- A cigar study in healthy young adults found "no significant difference in percent dilation after reactive hyperemia **at 5 hours**" — [Am Heart J](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0002870302275857). **Different product; the only time-resolved endothelial datapoint I retrieved, and it is a null at 5 h.**

### Inferences
- **For the index, the vascular domain must be modelled as two superposed components with very different time constants:**
  - An **acute spike** (AIx, PWV, BP, FMD) demonstrably present within 15 min of one cigarette (Mahmud & Feely) — this is the "live" component that makes the app feel real-time.
  - A **chronic offset** (reduced PP amplification, elevated AIx, attenuated brachial FMD) present in young chronic smokers even when brachial BP is normal (Mahmud & Feely; Suzuki 2020) — this is the component that recovers over **~1 to 24 months** (2025 review).
- **τ for the acute component is unknown and must be flagged.** Mahmud & Feely only measured out to 15 min, so the measured window is the *rise*, not the *decay*. The cigar null at 5 h is weak evidence that the acute endothelial effect is gone by then. A defensible placeholder is **τ_acute,vasc ≈ 30–60 min** by analogy with the haemodynamic recovery timeline (HR back to baseline by 30 min — see Q5), explicitly labelled as extrapolated. **Do not present a decay curve as measured.**
- **τ for the chronic component:** the 2025 review's "within one month … sustained up to 24 months" supports **τ_chronic,vasc ≈ 1–3 months**, which is the right order for a multi-week app narrative. Pick τ = 30 days if a single value is needed, flagged as derived from a review's qualitative statement rather than a fitted curve.
- **The Suzuki 2020 brachial-vs-carotid dissociation is a caution against over-claiming.** "Your arteries" is too broad; the evidenced claim in young adults concerns peripheral conduit-artery function.
- Amato's −0.88 to −1.17 FMD percentage points per cigarette gives a **per-cigarette dose term** for the vascular domain — the only per-cigarette quantity I verified in any domain. It should anchor the dose scaling.

### Gaps
- **Duration / decay time constant of the acute single-cigarette FMD drop — NOT FOUND.** This was an explicit ask. No retrieved study measured FMD serially after one cigarette. Candidate sources not reached: Celermajer's early work; the Amato 2013 full text's time-course data.
- **Johnson 2010 FMD deltas**: baseline FMD, 1-year change in sustained quitters vs continuing smokers, p-values, and the weight-gain confounder analysis — all unretrieved. The journal (JACC) is unconfirmed. **High-value fetch; this is the flagship cessation-FMD RCT.**
- Pooled effect sizes from the 2024 FMD meta-analysis — unretrieved.
- **No PWV/AIx recovery time-course with actual timepoints** was found — only the review's qualitative "within one month … up to 24 months".
- Whether FMD recovery is **complete** (to never-smoker levels) or only partial after 1 year: unknown from retrieved material.

## Q5. Heart rate, blood pressure and HRV: acute per-cigarette change, smoker–nonsmoker resting differences, and post-cessation recovery (wearable-ready values)

### Takeaway
This is the **most wearable-ready domain** and has the cleanest single recovery number in the literature: one week of abstinence lowers 24-h heart rate by **7.3 ± 1.0 bpm** and 24-h BP by **3.5/1.9 mmHg**, with pNN50 and HF rising and daytime LF/HF falling (Minami 1999, within-subject randomised-order crossover, n = 39 men). Acutely, one cigarette raises HR ~**6–15 bpm** with return to baseline by **~30 min**. HRV dose-response in the general population is **−9.8% SDNN and −8.9% RMSSD per 10 g tobacco/day** (CHRIS, n large).

### Cited Findings

**Post-cessation recovery — the anchor study**
- **Minami J, Ishimitsu T, Matsuoka H. "Effects of Smoking Cessation on Blood Pressure and Heart Rate Variability in Habitual Smokers." *Hypertension* 1999;33(1):586–590, DOI 10.1161/01.HYP.33.1.586.** Design: **39 normotensive male habitual smokers**; one week smoking and one week abstaining in **randomised order**; 24-h ambulatory BP, HR and ECG R-R intervals recorded on the last day of each period. Results: 24-h BP lower in the nonsmoking period by **3.5 ± 1.1 mmHg systolic** and **1.9 ± 0.7 mmHg diastolic**, while **nighttime BP did not differ significantly**; **24-h HR lower by 7.3 ± 1.0 bpm (P < 0.0001)**, with **daytime −9.2 ± 1.1 bpm** and **nighttime −3.7 bpm**; "The pNN50 and the 24-hour HF component were significantly higher in the nonsmoking period, and the daytime LF/HF ratio was significantly lower in the nonsmoking period" — [AHA Journals](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.hyp.33.1.586); [PDF](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/01.HYP.33.1.586)
- A later meta-analysis characterises this as "1 week of smoking cessation significantly lowered 24-h BP compared to continued smoking" — [Tob Induc Dis](https://www.tobaccoinduceddiseases.org/The-role-of-smoking-cessation-programs-in-lowering-blood-npressure-A-retrospective,142664,0,2.html)
- **Harte CB, Meston CM 2013, "Effects of Smoking Cessation on Heart Rate Variability Among Long-Term Male Smokers", *Int J Behav Med*, DOI 10.1007/s12529-013-9295-0, PMID 23397454.** "Successful quitters (**n = 20**), compared to those who relapsed (**n = 42**), displayed significantly higher **SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50, LF, and HF** at follow-up"; conclusion: "smoking cessation significantly enhances HRV in chronic male smokers, indicating improved autonomic modulation of the heart", and "these findings may be **primarily attributable to nicotine discontinuation rather than tobacco smoke discontinuation alone**" — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12529-013-9295-0); [author PDF](https://labs.la.utexas.edu/mestonlab/files/2014/10/2013-Harte-Meston-Smoking-Cessation.pdf). **Note: this is 2013, not the "Harte & Meston" undated reference in the brief; and comparing quitters to relapsers is not a randomised contrast.**
- **Dose-response in the general population (CHRIS study):** among current smokers, HRV fell with intensity, with reductions of **9.8% (SDNN)** and **8.9% (RMSSD)** per **10 g of tobacco smoked per day**; smoking cessation was associated with higher HRV levels — "Effects of smoking status, history and intensity on heart rate variability in the general population: The CHRIS study", *PLoS ONE* 2019, DOI 10.1371/journal.pone.0215053 — [PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0215053). **This is the best-powered dose term in the entire index.**
- E-cigarette and tobacco effects on HRV, case-control (2025) — [PMC13538581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13538581/) (content not retrieved)

**Acute per-cigarette haemodynamics**
- Observational ECG study: mean HR **~84 bpm before smoking → ~90.5 bpm during smoking**, "Heart rate rises within **10 min** of smoking and remained elevated for the next **20 min**", and "returned to baseline **after 30 min**" — "Acute electrocardiographic changes during smoking: an observational study" — [PMC3641479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3641479/)
- A review of nicotine cardiovascular toxicity puts the acute HR increase from smoking at **up to ~10–15 bpm, averaging ~7 bpm across the day**, and notes "Cigarette smoking causes a **>150% increase in plasma epinephrine**" — [PMC4958544, "Cardiovascular Toxicity of Nicotine: Implications for Electronic Cigarette Use"](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4958544/). **Authorship was not visible in retrieved results, so the brief's attribution to Benowitz is unconfirmed here (the paper is, to my knowledge, Benowitz & Burbank, Trends Cardiovasc Med 2016 — verify).**
- **Nicotine dose governs both magnitude and duration:** "the magnitude and duration of this increase was directly related to the nicotine content of the cigarettes smoked", with a high-nicotine cigarette's effect on cardiac output persisting for **one hour** — "Hemodynamic Effects of Smoking Cigarettes of High and Low Nicotine Content", *Chest* — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0012369215399207)
- Acute cardiovascular and sympathetic effects of nicotine replacement therapy (useful for separating nicotine from combustion effects in an NRT-using user) — [Hypertension, AHA PDF](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/pdf/10.1161/01.HYP.0000219284.47970.34)
- A challenge study reported only "approximately **4 beat per minute (bpm)** increase in heart rate after vaping or smoking, with no change in non-users" — [AHA newsroom](https://newsroom.heart.org/news/people-who-vape-had-worrisome-changes-in-cardiovascular-function-even-as-young-adults). **Conference-abstract-level; the smallest of the retrieved estimates.**

**Resting HR, smokers vs nonsmokers (cross-sectional baselines)**
- **CARDIA (large cohort of healthy young adults):** "heart rate at rest was significantly higher in smokers by **1.5 to 5 beats/min** in all race/gender groups **except black men**" — "Cardiac function in smokers and nonsmokers: the CARDIA study", [PubMed 7797754](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/7797754/). **Best-quality cross-sectional estimate retrieved, and the subgroup exception is worth respecting in product copy.**
- **Papathanasiou G et al. 2013, "Effects of Smoking on Heart Rate at Rest and During Exercise, and on Heart Rate Recovery, in Young Adults", *Hellenic J Cardiol* 54(3):168–177.** **298 young adults (159 men), aged 20–29**, maximal Bruce treadmill test. Smokers had significantly higher resting HR; both female and male smokers showed significantly **slower HR increase during exercise**; **female smokers failed to reach age-predicted maximum HR by 6.0 bpm and males by 3.6 bpm**; HR recovery decline was significantly attenuated **only in female smokers** — [Hellenic J Cardiol PDF](https://www.hellenicjcardiol.org/archive/full_text/2013/3/2013_3_168.pdf); [institutional mirror](http://hypatia.teiath.gr/xmlui/bitstream/handle/11400/4900/EFFECTS%20OF%20SMOKING%20ON%20HEART%20RATE%20AT%20REST%20AND%20DURING%20EXERCISE,%20AND%20ON%20HEART%20RATE%20RECOVERY,%20IN%20YOUNG%20ADULTS.pdf?sequence=1). **This is an exact age match (20–29) for the target user.**
- Small cross-sectional studies: resting HR **63.05 bpm (non-smokers) vs 68.32 bpm (smokers)**, men aged 18–25, n = 50 — [Academia.edu record](https://www.academia.edu/99365791/Resting_Heart_Rate_and_Exercise_Heart_Rate_of_Adult_Men_Smokers_and_Non_smokers_A_Comparative_Study); and **75 ± 3 bpm (smokers, n = 5) vs 64 ± 5 bpm (nonsmokers, n = 7)** — [PMC7548394](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7548394/). **Both very small; the second has n = 5.**

### Inferences
- **Wearable-ready parameter set (all sourced):**
  | Quantity | Value | Source quality |
  |---|---|---|
  | Acute ΔHR per cigarette | +6.5 bpm measured (84→90.5); up to +10–15 bpm per review | observational / review |
  | Acute ΔHR return to baseline | **~30 min** (up to ~60 min for high-nicotine) | observational |
  | Resting HR excess in young smokers | **+1.5 to +5 bpm** (CARDIA, large) | good |
  | 24-h HR fall after 1 week abstinence | **−7.3 ± 1.0 bpm** (daytime −9.2, night −3.7) | good (randomised crossover, n=39 men) |
  | 24-h BP fall after 1 week abstinence | **−3.5/−1.9 mmHg** (night NS) | good |
  | HRV dose-response | **−9.8% SDNN, −8.9% RMSSD per 10 g/day** | good (population) |
  | HRV after quitting | ↑ SDNN, RMSSD, pNN50, LF, HF in quitters vs relapsers | moderate (non-randomised) |
- **τ for the autonomic domain is bounded but not measured.** Minami shows the effect is essentially complete at 1 week; Minami does **not** report intermediate days, so the within-week trajectory is unknown. A defensible placeholder is **τ_auto ≈ 2–3 days** (giving ~90–95% recovery by day 7), explicitly labelled as an engineering interpolation between the 30-min acute decay and the 7-day endpoint. **This is the clearest case in the index where a smooth curve will be shown on the strength of two endpoints.**
- **Striking internal consistency worth exploiting in product:** the 1-week abstinence ΔHR (−7.3 bpm) is nearly identical to the review's estimate of the *average* day-long HR elevation from smoking (~7 bpm). Independent methods converging on ~7 bpm makes "about 7 beats per minute of your resting heart rate is the cigarettes" a defensible, memorable claim — and it is directly checkable against the user's own wearable, which is a strong engagement loop.
- **The night/day asymmetry is a product constraint.** Minami found BP *night* differences non-significant and the night HR effect (−3.7 bpm) less than half the daytime effect (−9.2 bpm). Many consumer wearables derive "resting HR" from **sleep**. An app reading sleeping HR will therefore see roughly **half** the effect that a 24-h average would show — so the expected effect size must be set against the sleep-derived metric, or the index will look under-responsive.
- **Sex and subgroup caveats are real, not boilerplate:** Minami and Harte & Meston are **male-only**; Papathanasiou found HR recovery attenuation **only in women**; CARDIA found no resting-HR excess in black men. The index should not present identical expected trajectories to all users.

### Gaps
- **Yotsukura 1998** (cited in the brief) — not retrieved.
- **No weeks-to-months cohort tracking resting HR after quitting was found.** The longest within-subject cessation data retrieved is 1 week (Minami). The "RHR drop over days–weeks" framing in the brief is therefore evidenced at 1 week and **extrapolated beyond it**.
- A 1996 nicotine-patch trial reportedly found HRV still below healthy middle-aged reference values 4 weeks after all nicotine cessation — **this appeared in search summary without a resolvable citation and is deliberately excluded from the Cited Findings.** It matters (it would imply incomplete HRV recovery at 1 month) and should be re-found.
- **Per-cigarette HRV decrement and its recovery τ**: no acute HRV time-course retrieved.
- Absolute SDNN/RMSSD values (ms) in young smokers vs nonsmokers — needed to calibrate a wearable display — not retrieved; CHRIS gives only percentage differences.
- Whether HRV recovery is complete vs partial at 1, 3 and 12 months: unknown.

## Q6. Small-airway inflammation and exercise capacity in young smokers; FeNO

### Takeaway
Two well-evidenced, FEV1-independent stories exist for young smokers: (1) **oscillometry detects small-airway dysfunction when spirometry (including FEF25-75) is normal** — a 2025 AJRCCM paper states subjects with preserved-airflow SAD "had normal expiratory flow, even at mid to low lung volumes (FEF25–75%)", which **undercuts FEF25-75 as the app's small-airway marker**; and (2) **FeNO is suppressed in smokers and rises after quitting, approximating nonsmoker values by ~4–8 weeks** — though a 3-month study found no significant FeNO change, a direct conflict. VO2max deficit magnitudes in young smokers were **not** retrieved.

### Cited Findings

**Small-airway dysfunction with preserved spirometry**
- **2025, AJRCCM: "Oscillometry-defined Small Airway Dysfunction in Tobacco-exposed Adults with Impaired or Preserved Airflow", DOI 10.1164/rccm.202501-0028oc.** Subjects with preserved-airflow SAD "had **normal expiratory flow, even at mid to low lung volumes (FEF25–75%)**"; "spirometry has **poor sensitivity for SAD**, especially when FEV1 and FVC are within normal limits" — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1164/rccm.202501-0028oc). **Most recent and most decision-relevant finding in this section.**
- **Asymptomatic smokers with preserved spirometry, impulse oscillometry (IOS):** n = 75, mean age **47 ± 12 y**, **26 ± 17 pack-years**. Pack-years correlated with both spirometric and oscillometric indices (**FVC, FEV1, FEV1/FVC, FEV3/FEV6, FEF25-75** and **R5-R20, AX, Fres**). Conclusion: "impulse oscillometry can detect small airway dysfunction in asymptomatic heavy smokers with preserved spirometry and with FEV3/FEV6 values in the normal range." Mild-smoker subgroup: **n = 39, mean 14.5 pack-years, age ~41** — "Detection of Small Airway Dysfunction in Asymptomatic Smokers with Preserved Spirometry: The Value of the Impulse Oscillometry System", *Int J COPD*, DOI 10.2147/COPD.S319972 — [PMC8449545](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8449545); [Dovepress](https://www.dovepress.com/detection-of-small-airway-dysfunction-in-asymptomatic-smokers-with-pre-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-COPD). **Population caveat: mean age 47 and 26 pack-years — NOT the app's 20–25-year-old, 2-year-history user.**
- SAD is commonly defined by "FEF25-75 value less than **60% or 65% of predicted**, depending on the clinical setting" — [Dovepress](https://www.dovepress.com/detection-of-small-airway-dysfunction-in-asymptomatic-smokers-with-pre-peer-reviewed-fulltext-article-COPD)
- In 80 smokers with normal spirometry assessed by multiple-breath nitrogen washout and oscillometry: "abnormal peripheral and global concavity as well as **acinar ventilation heterogeneity are common in 'normal' smokers**", but concavity measures "were **not** related to either MBNW or IOS measurements" — [PMC9145514](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9145514/). **Internal inconsistency between markers; argues against treating "small airway dysfunction" as one number.**
- SAD impairs quality of life among smokers with no airflow limitation — [Arch Bronconeumol](https://www.archbronconeumol.org/en-small-airway-dysfunction-impairs-quality-articulo-S1579212919303489). **This is the kind of patient-relevant, FEV1-independent endpoint the index wants.**
- 2026 pilot: forced oscillation technique parameters, "particularly **R5**, demonstrated comparable diagnostic accuracy with spirometry in smokers" — [PubMed 41556836](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41556836/)

**Exercise capacity in young smokers**
- **2025, MSSE: "Cigarette Smoking Impairs Cardiorespiratory and Metabolic Response at Peak Incremental Exercise and during Recovery in Young, Physically Active Adults", DOI 10.1249/MSS.0000000000003602** — exact population match (young, physically active) — [Ovid](https://www.ovid.com/jnls/acsm-msse/fulltext/10.1249/mss.0000000000003602~cigarette-smoking-impairs-cardiorespiratory-and-metabolic); [PMC11878581](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11878581/). **Numbers not retrieved — highest-priority fetch for this section.**
- Companion paper: "Cigarette smoking **slows the on- and off- cardiorespiratory and gas-exchange kinetics** during moderate exercise in young, physically active adults" — [PMC13380561](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13380561/). **Kinetics-based (τ of VO2 on/off response) — conceptually ideal for a kinetics-driven index. Numbers not retrieved.**
- Papathanasiou G et al. 2007, "Effects of chronic smoking on exercise tolerance and on heart rate–systolic blood pressure product in young healthy adults", ***Eur J Cardiovasc Prev Rehabil* 14(5):646–652** (**not** Hellenic J Cardiol, correcting the brief). Summarised by a later review as finding "chronic smoking was found to affect young male smokers' cardiovascular fitness, impairing the economy and decreasing the capacity of their circulatory system" — secondary summary via [Hellenic J Cardiol 2013 PDF](https://www.hellenicjcardiol.org/archive/full_text/2013/3/2013_3_168.pdf)
- Nicotine dependence and cardiopulmonary endurance in college students (Henan, China) — [Tob Induc Dis](https://www.tobaccoinduceddiseases.org/Nicotine-dependence-affects-cardiopulmonary-endurance-and-nphysical-activity-in-college,166133,0,2.html). **Numbers not retrieved.**
- Exercise HR ceiling deficit in 20–29-year-olds: female smokers **−6.0 bpm** and male smokers **−3.6 bpm** below age-predicted maximum HR — Papathanasiou 2013 (full details in Q5) — [Hellenic J Cardiol PDF](https://www.hellenicjcardiol.org/archive/full_text/2013/3/2013_3_168.pdf)

**FeNO**
- **Quitting raises exhaled NO on a ~1–8 week timescale:** 14 smokers who stayed abstinent had exhaled NO above baseline at **1 week** while 5 who relapsed did not; "by **eight weeks**, exhaled NO levels approximated those of normal nonsmoking control subjects" — "Smoking Cessation Is Associated With an Increase in Exhaled Nitric Oxide", *Chest* — [ScienceDirect](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0012369215529966). **n = 19 total.**
- 9 smokers who stopped for 4 weeks: airway NO production was decreased in smokers but "could be **restored to normal values by four weeks** after cessation" — "Increased nitric oxide elimination from the airways after smoking cessation", *Clin Sci* 2002;103(1):15 — [Portland Press](https://portlandpress.com/clinsci/article-abstract/103/1/15/67355/Increased-Nitric-Oxide-elimination-from-the). **n = 9.**
- **DIRECT CONTRADICTION:** "FeNO, nitrotyrosine and MMP-8 levels **did not change significantly** during the three months after smoking cessation" — "Persistence of oxidant and protease burden in the airways after smoking cessation" — [PMC2697135](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC2697135/)
- Reduced-nicotine-content cigarettes "**attenuated smoking-related reductions in FeNO** levels in smokers with affective disorders and socioeconomic disadvantage", though not in those with opioid use disorder, and FeNO changes "were **not accompanied by changes in respiratory-health ratings**" — *Nicotine Tob Res* 2021, DOI 10.1093/ntr/ntab145 — [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/ntr/ntab145); [PMC8826384](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8826384/)
- 1-year randomised cessation trial of e-cigarettes measured breathomics changes — [PubMed 27322745](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27322745/)

### Inferences
- **Drop FEF25-75 as the app's small-airway marker.** The 2025 AJRCCM finding that preserved-airflow SAD subjects have *normal* FEF25-75 means the brief's proposed marker will be insensitive precisely in the target population. **Oscillometry (R5-R20, AX, Fres, R5) is the evidenced modality** — but it requires clinic hardware a phone app cannot access, so this domain is **informational/narrative, not live-measurable**. Treat it as context, not as an index component with a live value.
- **The FeNO evidence is genuinely conflicted and the studies are tiny (n = 9, n = 19).** Two small studies say normalisation by 4–8 weeks; one says no change at 3 months. **Do not put FeNO in the index as a quantitative domain.** If used at all, use it as a qualitative "airway inflammatory signalling begins to normalise in the first weeks (small studies; one study found no change)" line. The "FeNO paradox" named in the brief — that smoking *lowers* a marker normally *elevated* by inflammation — is confirmed in direction by these sources and is worth explaining, because a naive user seeing FeNO rise after quitting would misread it as worsening.
- **The exercise-capacity domain is the single biggest motivational opportunity and my weakest evidence.** For a 20–25-year-old, "you get out of breath on the stairs sooner" is more motivating than any biomarker, and two 2025 papers in *exactly* this population exist — but I retrieved **no effect sizes**. The VO2max deficit figures the brief asked for (Papathanasiou, Bernaards 2003, military recruit studies) are **not** in my results. **Flag: no VO2max number may be shipped from this pass.**
- The "slowed on-/off- gas-exchange kinetics" paper is conceptually the best fit for a kinetics index — it describes smoking's effect *as a time constant* — and should be the first fetch in any follow-up.

### Gaps
- **VO2max deficit in young smokers vs nonsmokers: NO NUMBER RETRIEVED.** Papathanasiou 2007, Bernaards 2003, military-recruit studies, and Song et al. were not reached.
- **Fitness gains after quitting: no data retrieved at all.** Not even a direction-of-effect source.
- Effect sizes from the two 2025 young-adult exercise papers (MSSE and the gas-exchange kinetics paper) — unretrieved.
- Oscillometry values (R5-R20, AX in absolute units) **in young, low-pack-year smokers specifically** — the available IOS study is mean age 47, 26 pack-years. **No retrieved study matches a 22-year-old with 2 years of smoking.** This is a true evidence gap for the target population, not just retrieval failure.
- **Whether small-airway dysfunction in young light smokers is reversible, and over what time course**: nothing retrieved. Any recovery curve for this domain would be pure invention.
- Elevated airway inflammatory cell counts (BAL neutrophils/macrophages) in young light smokers, and their post-cessation trajectory: not retrieved. Note the oxidant/protease persistence paper above suggests **some** airway inflammatory markers do **not** normalise within 3 months — a caution against promising rapid airway-inflammation recovery.
- Tashkin / Wan / Kirkham "small airways disease in young smokers" specifically: not reached.

## Q7. Taste and smell recovery; bronchial epithelial mutational/clonal "replenishment" as a narrative

### Takeaway
Smell recovery is **real, dose-dependent and slow**: measurable improvement in odour discrimination/identification at **45 days** (n = 28), but former smokers who quit **≤15 years** prior still had significantly impaired olfaction vs never-smokers. The Yoshida 2020 Nature finding is the strongest "healthy cells come back" narrative available: near-normal cells are **~4-fold more abundant in ex-smokers than current smokers** and carry **longer telomeres**, while smoking adds **~1,000–10,000 mutations per cell**.

### Cited Findings

**Smell (and taste)**
- Frye RE, Schwartz BS, Doty RL. "Dose-Related Effects of Cigarette Smoking on Olfactory Function." *JAMA* 1990. "Among previous smokers, improvement in olfactory function was related to the **time elapsed since the cessation of smoking**"; conclusion: "smoking causes **long-term but reversible** adverse effects on the ability to smell" — [JAMA Network](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jama/article-abstract/380894). A review summarises it as "restoration of smell function to the level observed in non-smokers requires **approximately the same duration as the number of years smoked**" — **this is a SECONDARY characterisation, not a quote from the paper, and should be verified before use** — [ResearchGate review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/5763751_The_effect_of_smoking_on_the_olfactory_function)
- A meta-analysis notes Frye et al. found "a significant **dose-response relationship between pack-years smoked and score on the UPSIT** among current and former smokers" — "Smoking and Olfactory Dysfunction: a Systematic Literature Review and Meta-Analysis" — [PMC6731037](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6731037/)
- **Fastest measured improvement:** in 28 quitters, "significant improvement in **odor discrimination, odor identification, and TDI scores 45 days after smoking cessation**", with improvement **inversely associated with the duration of smoking** — "Smoking cessation improves olfactory functions" — [PubMed 30953390](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30953390/). **n = 28; this is the key datapoint for a weeks-scale smell narrative, and the inverse association with smoking duration FAVOURS the app's young, short-history user.**
- **Long-run incompleteness:** former smokers who quit **≤15 years** before testing had significantly impaired olfaction vs never-smokers, while those who quit **>15 years** prior did not — Siegel et al. 2019, "Olfactory dysfunction persists after smoking cessation and signals increased cardiovascular risk", *Int Forum Allergy Rhinol*, DOI 10.1002/alr.22357 — [Wiley](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alr.22357)
- **CONTRADICTION:** a Japanese study found "the time since quitting cigarette smoking **did not** exhibit a relation with the CC-SIT score" — "Effects of smoking on odour identification in Japanese subjects" — [PubMed 17956024](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17956024/)
- Olfactory outcomes are also being measured in a modern randomised cessation/ENDS trial (ESTxENDS substudy) — [ClinicalTrials.gov NCT04617444](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT04617444)

**Bronchial epithelial clonal replenishment (Yoshida 2020)**
- **Yoshida K, Gowers KHC, et al. "Tobacco smoking and somatic mutations in human bronchial epithelium." *Nature* 2020;578(7794):266–272, DOI 10.1038/s41586-020-1961-1, PMID 31996850** (Wellcome Sanger Institute / UCL). Method: "whole genomes of **632 colonies** derived from single bronchial epithelial cells across **16 subjects**", recruited at clinically indicated bronchoscopy, including children, never-smokers, ex-smokers and current smokers. Smoking was the dominant influence on mutation load, adding roughly **1,000 to 10,000 mutations per cell**. A subset of cells in people with a smoking history carried mutation burdens similar to never-smokers; these were "about **fourfold more common in ex-smokers than in current smokers**" and had **longer telomeres** than the more-mutated cells. Driver mutations rose with age, affecting **4–14% of cells in middle-aged never-smokers**, and **at least 25% of cells in current smokers**. Authors concluded that quitting supports renewal of the bronchial lining from **mitotically quiescent cells that escaped tobacco mutagenesis** — [PubMed 31996850](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31996850/); [PMC7021511](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7021511/); [UCL Discovery](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10091148/); [accepted manuscript PDF](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/10091148/3/Janes_Bronchial%20epithelium_FINAL_no_ref_fields.pdf)
- Press framing: "Individuals Who Stop Smoking at Any Age Experience Regeneration of Lung Cells" — [CancerNetwork](https://www.cancernetwork.com/individuals-who-stop-smoking-any-age-experience-regeneration-lung-cells)

### Inferences
- **Smell is an excellent BTI domain for this user: it is subjectively noticeable, has a weeks-scale first signal (45 days), and its recovery is *faster* the shorter the smoking history** (Frye's dose-response; the 45-day study's inverse association with duration). A 2-year, 22-year-old smoker is the best-case recovery profile — which inverts the usual "young smokers have nothing to show" problem and should be said explicitly in copy.
- But **the honest framing is "partial recovery over weeks, full recovery over years"**: Siegel's ≤15-year threshold shows the deficit is long-lived in heavier/longer histories. The app should not promise normalisation.
- **Yoshida 2020 should be a narrative module, NOT an index domain.** The fourfold enrichment is cross-sectional (ex-smokers vs current smokers, n = 16 subjects total), the paper reports **no time constant** for clonal replacement, and the process operates on years. Modelling it as a live exponential would be fabrication. Its value is rhetorical and it is unusually strong rhetoric: *the healthy cells were already there, waiting, protected from mutagenesis, and quitting lets them repopulate the lining.* That is a mechanism, not a risk statistic, and it is exactly the register that works for a user who dismisses "lung age".
- **Taste specifically has no evidence in my results.** Every retrieved source is olfactory (UPSIT, CC-SIT, TDI). Since flavour perception is largely olfactory, the common "taste and smell return in days" claim is probably a conflation. **The app should say "smell" and cite the 45-day study; it should not claim a days-scale taste recovery.**

### Gaps
- **Taste (gustatory) recovery after cessation: no sourced data.** The brief's "taste/smell recovery (days)" timescale is **unsupported** — the fastest sourced olfactory improvement is 45 days, not days.
- **Yoshida 2020 quantitative details not retrieved:** mutations per cell per year in never-smokers, additional mutations per pack-year, and whether near-normal cell fraction scales with *years since quitting* (which would be the only basis for any time constant). The paper abstract gives the fourfold ex- vs current contrast but no kinetics.
- Frye 1990 full text — needed to confirm or kill the secondary "recovery takes as long as you smoked" claim, which is currently the most quotable and least verified statement in this section.
- No study retrieved measures smell recovery at intermediate points between 45 days and 15 years, so the shape of the curve is unknown.

## Q8. Proposed composite index: explicit formula, per-domain accumulation/clearance models, weights, and evidence grading

### Takeaway
A defensible BTI can be built **today** from three well-evidenced live domains (CO, nicotine, autonomic) plus two medium-evidence multi-week domains (mucociliary, vascular), with smell as a slow partial-recovery domain and clonal replenishment as narrative only. **Only two per-cigarette dose terms in the entire literature review were verifiable** (ΔFMD −0.88 to −1.17 pp; the CHRIS HRV −9.8%/−8.9% per 10 g/day), so the dose scaling of most domains must be exposed as a calibrated parameter rather than a published constant.

### Cited Findings
*(This section is design synthesis; the kinetic parameters below carry the citations established in Q1–Q7. Repeating the load-bearing ones:)*
- CO/COHb decay: mono-exponential, **t½ 4.5 h daytime (r² = 0.7)** — [Sandberg 2011, PLoS ONE](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0028864); sleep **t½ 8.0 ± 1.6 h men (n = 5) / 4.3 ± 1.1 h women (n = 4)** — [PubMed 1498552](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/1498552/)
- Nicotine **t½ ≈ 2 h** — [secondary, citing Benowitz/Hukkanen/Jacob 2009](https://arupconsult.com/reference/19184645)
- Autonomic: acute ΔHR resolves by **~30 min** — [PMC3641479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3641479/); **−7.3 ± 1.0 bpm** 24-h HR and **−3.5/−1.9 mmHg** BP after **1 week** abstinence — [Minami 1999, Hypertension](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/10.1161/01.hyp.33.1.586); dose-response **−9.8% SDNN / −8.9% RMSSD per 10 g tobacco/day** — [CHRIS, PLoS ONE 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0215053)
- Mucociliary: STT normal by **day 15**, sustained to **180 days** — [Ramos 2011, Respirology](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1440-1843.2011.01985.x); magnitude ≈ **16 min vs 8–10 min** — [Ito 2015, Respir Care](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/full/10.4187/respcare.03266)
- Vascular: acute AIx/PWV/BP rise within **15 min** of one cigarette — [Mahmud & Feely 2003, Hypertension](https://www.ahajournals.org/doi/full/10.1161/01.hyp.0000047464.66901.60); **ΔFMD −0.88 to −1.17 pp per cigarette** — [Amato 2013](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23528831/); recovery "within one month … sustained up to 24 months", moderate certainty — [2025 systematic review](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/vascular-health-after-quitting-smoking-or-switching-to-e-cigarett/)
- Olfactory: significant TDI improvement at **45 days** (n = 28) — [PubMed 30953390](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30953390/); still impaired in those who quit **≤15 years** ago — [Siegel 2019](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/alr.22357)

### Inferences

**Proposed formula.**

`BTI(t) = Σ_k w_k · D_k(t)`, with each `D_k ∈ [0,1]` (0 = never-smoker state, 1 = steady-state burden of a heavy smoker), and `Σ w_k = 1`.

Each domain is a dose-driven accumulation with first-order clearance:

`D_k(t) = min( 1 , Σ_i a_k(CPD) · exp( −(t − t_i)/τ_k ) + b_k(t) )`

- `a_k(CPD)` — per-cigarette increment for domain k. **Only the vascular and (population-level) HRV terms have published dose anchors.** For all others, expose `a_k` as a parameter calibrated so that a user's declared CPD yields the published *steady-state* smoker–nonsmoker gap, and say so in the app's methods page.
- `b_k(t)` — slow chronic offset, itself relaxing with a long time constant `T_k` after the quit date: `b_k(t) = b_k(0) · exp(−(t − t_quit)/T_k)`.
- `τ_k` — fast (within-day) clearance; `T_k` — slow (weeks–months) structural/functional recovery.

**Per-domain parameter table.** "Source" = provenance of the time constant; "Grade" = my evidence grade (A = primary, adequately powered, population-appropriate; B = primary but small/indirect/wrong population; C = secondary or extrapolated; D = unsourced, do not ship).

| k | Domain | Live? | τ_k (fast) | T_k (slow) | Dose anchor | Grade | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| CO | Carboxyhaemoglobin / exhaled CO | **Yes** | **6.49 h** (t½ 4.5 h, awake); ×~1.5–1.8 asleep | n/a (no chronic offset) | **none verified** | τ: **A**; dose: **D** | Only domain with a primary, population-matched decay fit. Sleep multiplier is **B** (n<6). |
| NIC | Nicotine (urge channel) | Yes (modelled) | **2.89 h** (t½ 2 h) | n/a | none verified | τ: **C** (secondary citation); dose: **D** | Verify against the Benowitz chapter. Cotinine arm **D** — half-life unverified. |
| AUTO | Heart rate / HRV / BP | **Yes (wearable)** | **~0.72 h** (acute ΔHR gone by ~30 min) | **~2–3 days** (engineering interpolation to Minami's 1-week endpoint) | **−9.8% SDNN, −8.9% RMSSD per 10 g/day** | τ_fast: **B**; T: **C**; dose: **A** | Best live domain. Expect ~half the effect size if read from *sleeping* HR. Male-only recovery data. |
| MCC | Mucociliary clearance | No (narrative + date-driven) | n/a | **~5–7 days** chosen so ~85–95% recovery by **day 15** | none | T: **B** (single-arm n=33; endpoint only) | Published data are a **step at day 15**, not a curve. Curve shape is ours. |
| VASC | Endothelial / arterial stiffness | No (narrative) | **~0.5–1 h** (extrapolated; only the 15-min rise is measured) | **~30 days** (from "within one month…24 months") | **−0.88 to −1.17 pp FMD per cigarette** | τ_fast: **C**; T: **C**; dose: **A** | Strongest *chronic, young-adult, FEV1-independent* abnormality (Mahmud & Feely). |
| OLF | Smell | No (self-report) | n/a | **~45 days** for first significant improvement; incomplete for years | none | T: **B** (n=28) | Recovers faster with shorter history — favours target user. Do **not** claim taste. |
| SAD | Small-airway dysfunction | No | n/a | **unknown** | none | **D** | No reversibility data, no young-light-smoker norms. **Narrative only; no live value.** |
| GEN | Clonal/mutational replenishment | No | n/a | years; **no published time constant** | n/a | narrative only | Yoshida 2020. Never animate as a curve. |

**Recommended weights** (rationale, not evidence — these are a design choice):
- `w_CO = 0.30`, `w_AUTO = 0.25`, `w_NIC = 0.15`, `w_VASC = 0.15`, `w_MCC = 0.10`, `w_OLF = 0.05`; SAD and GEN weight 0.
- Rationale: weight is allocated by **(evidence grade) × (measurability) × (within-horizon responsiveness)**. CO and AUTO dominate because they are the only domains that are both primary-evidenced *and* observable live (breath CO device / wearable). NIC is weighted for its explanatory role in craving rather than toxicity. VASC gets meaningful weight because it is the one domain with a **chronic abnormality demonstrated in young adults with normal brachial BP** — i.e. it directly solves the "nothing is wrong with me yet" problem. MCC and OLF are low-weight because their curves are our interpolations between sparse endpoints. SAD and GEN are zero-weighted precisely because an honest index cannot animate them.
- **Mixed-timescale caution:** CO/NIC (hours) and MCC/VASC/OLF (weeks) in one scalar means the headline number will move fast then stall. **Recommend displaying two numbers — an "acute load" (CO+NIC+AUTO-fast) and a "recovery progress" (MCC+VASC+OLF+AUTO-slow) — rather than one BTI.** A single scalar will read as broken when the hours-scale terms bottom out on day 2 and nothing visibly changes for a fortnight.

**Honest-labelling requirement.** Every domain should carry a visible provenance badge. On my grading, **only CO's τ, the CHRIS HRV dose term, the Amato FMD dose term, and Minami's 1-week autonomic endpoint reach grade A**. Everything else is B, C, or D. Shipping B/C curves is fine; shipping them *unlabelled* is not, and a D-grade number (per-cigarette ΔCOHb, cotinine τ, any VO2max figure, any SAD recovery curve) must not ship at all from this pass.

### Gaps
- **Nine of the ~14 parameters needed for the full model are unverified** (per-cigarette ΔCOHb; cotinine τ; exercise effect on CO τ; acute FMD decay τ; intermediate MCC trajectory; VO2max deficit and its recovery; SAD reversibility; taste recovery; clonal replacement kinetics).
- No published composite index of this kind was found for comparison or validation — **I found no reliable source for any existing "reversible toxicity" composite score in smoking cessation.** The BTI is novel, which also means it is unvalidated: no data exist on whether such an index predicts quit outcomes.
- The weights above have **no empirical basis** — no study ranks these domains by motivational impact. They should be treated as a v1 prior to be revised against in-app engagement data.
- **Validation path unaddressed:** nothing retrieved speaks to whether showing users modelled biomarker recovery improves abstinence. (Related: the SRNT 2020 update notes its own cutoff recommendations rest on narrative synthesis rather than outcome data — [J Environ Expo Assess 2025](https://www.oaepublish.com/articles/jeea.2025.69) — a useful precedent for how much of this field is consensus rather than measurement.)

- ALA uses "**1 to 9 Months** After Quitting" — [ALA](https://www.lung.org/quit-smoking/i-want-to-quit/benefits-of-quitting)
- The cilia clause ("cilia … start to regain normal function in the lungs") appears in secondary consumer sources citing the 1990 report's pages; a public-health handout "adapted from" that report hedges to "**potential for** cilia to regrow in lungs" — [WDMH handout](https://www.wdmh.on.ca/uploads/Common/1791_Health%20Benefits%20of%20Quitting%20Smoking_Accessible.pdf). **WHO's own rendering omits cilia entirely** — [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/questions-and-answers/item/tobacco-health-benefits-of-smoking-cessation)
- Variant timings circulate in secondary sources (e.g. cilia "largely restored by 3–9 months"; "within a year … lung function returns to nearly normal") — see e.g. [Orlando Health content hub](https://www.orlandohealth.com/content-hub/how-the-lungs-heal-after-quitting-smoking), [Healthline](https://www.healthline.com/health/quit-smoking/pictures-of-lungs-after-quitting-smoking). **Low-quality; listed only to document the dispersion.**
- **Independent primary support for two of the early milestones exists in this research pass and is stronger than the timeline itself:**
  - The "**20 minutes — heart rate drops**" entry is consistent with the measured acute HR course: rise within 10 min, elevated for the next 20 min, baseline by 30 min — [PMC3641479](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3641479/). **Note this supports ~30 min, not 20 min.**
  - The "**8–12 hours — CO normalises**" entry is consistent with t½ 4.5 h (≈ 2 half-lives = ~75% cleared in 9 h), i.e. **substantially cleared but not normalised** — [Sandberg 2011](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0028864)
  - The "**2 wk–3 mo — circulation / lung function improves**" entry is consistent with the independent MCC (day 15 — [Ramos 2011](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1440-1843.2011.01985.x)) and vascular ("within one month" — [2025 review](https://discovery.dundee.ac.uk/en/publications/vascular-health-after-quitting-smoking-or-switching-to-e-cigarett/)) findings above.

### Inferences
- **The canonical timeline is a guideline-consensus artefact, not a set of measurements, and should be labelled as such in the app.** Evidence: two authoritative bodies give different upper bounds for the same entry (9 vs 12 months); the cilia clause has accreted onto the 1990 attribution without appearing in WHO's version; and secondary sources vary freely. Presenting it as a precise clock is the kind of over-claim that a sceptical 22-year-old can disprove in one search — exactly the credibility failure the BTI is meant to avoid.
- **Where the app can do better: three of the early milestones can be re-grounded in primary kinetics from this research pass** (30-min HR return; CO t½ 4.5 h; day-15 MCC), with real citations and real uncertainty. That is a genuine product advantage over every competitor reciting the 1990 list.
- **The late milestones (1 yr CHD halved, 5 yr stroke, 10 yr lung cancer halved, 15 yr CHD = nonsmoker) are epidemiological risk projections, not reversible physiology, and are outside the BTI's remit.** They are also the entries most likely to demotivate the target user (a 22-year-old does not act on a 15-year CHD horizon). Recommend relegating them to a static "long-term" page, cited to the Surgeon General reports, and keeping them out of the index.
- The Yoshida 2020 clonal-replenishment mechanism (Q7) is a **better** long-horizon narrative than the "10 yr lung cancer risk halved" statistic, because it supplies a *mechanism* for why risk falls rather than an actuarial number.

### Gaps
- **Not individually verified:** 20 min (HR/BP), 8–12 h (CO/oxygen), 2 wk–3 mo (circulation/lung function), 1 yr (CHD risk halved), 5 yr (stroke), 10 yr (lung cancer death rate halved), 15 yr (CHD = never-smoker). I read only the 1–9 month entry verbatim.
- **No Surgeon General report was opened.** USDHHS 1990 (*The Health Benefits of Smoking Cessation*, CDC publication 90-8416), 2010, 2014, and 2020 (*Smoking Cessation*) were all unreachable (cdc.gov DNS failure). **Page-level tracing of each milestone remains to be done**, and the 2020 report is the one that would satisfy the brief's 2020–2026 recency requirement for this question.
- Primary epidemiological sources behind the 1-, 5-, 10- and 15-year risk milestones (e.g. the cohort studies the 1990 report synthesised): not reached.
- Whether the 2020 Surgeon General report **revises** any of the 1990 timings: unknown, and worth checking, since the app would otherwise cite a 36-year-old synthesis.
